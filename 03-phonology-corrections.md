This number is also the motivational payoff offered against week-1 boredom, so overselling it is expensive when it fails. Drop the figure; the unit-5 argument does not need it.\n\n### 2.12 \"Grapheme-to-phoneme conversion is automatic and uninhibitable\" [2 lenses, one of them self-defeating]\n\nAutomaticity of L1 G2P is well supported; *uninhibitability* is not — literate bilinguals routinely switch mappings. Worse, the claim defeats its own conclusion: if G2P is uninhibitable it fires on silent reading too, and the plan puts a romaji string on the back of 6,000 cards to be silently read hundreds of times each — the same high-volume rehearsal of English phonology the plan bans in shadowing. \"Never aloud\" is a pragmatic heuristic dressed as a mechanism.\n\n**Adjudication.** Do not remove romaji — the learner needs to talk about words in English and to key input, and a bespoke glyph alphabet is machinery this learner does not need. Instead: drop the automaticity claim and admit \"never read romaji aloud\" is risk management; make the *integer beat count* and the *audio* the graded fields, with romaji demoted to a secondary/disambiguation position. The stronger argument for adding a notation field is not G2P at all — it is that romaji cannot encode syllable structure, which the plan's own pitch rules run on (see 3.1).\n\n### 2.13 Smaller hedges\n\n- **\"The r-tap IS the t in water\"** — true intervocalically, which is the only case drilled first. Word-initially Japanese /r/ varies toward a brief [d]-like or lateral contact, and English flapping is positionally restricted and not under conscious control. Rewrite: \"In the middle of a word it *is* the t in water. Word-initially it is the same gesture launched from silence — which is why the a-raku→raku scaffold is doing real work, not just removing a crutch.\" Also: \"twenty/thirty minutes buys the entire item\" is optimistic and unmeasured, and the /r/-/d/ perceptual contrast the unit itself identifies as the real problem is not a 30-minute item.\n- **\"English is stress-timed: interstress intervals held roughly constant\"** — strict isochrony did not survive measurement (Roach 1982, Dauer 1983). Replace with \"English compresses and centralizes unstressed vowels and tolerates complex syllable margins; Japanese does neither\" — truer, and a better fit to the pedagogy, since vowel reduction is the mechanism actually being fought.\n- **\"Roughly half of short Japanese nouns are heiban\"** [2 lenses] — heiban's share is length-conditioned. It is the *long* nouns, especially 4-mora Sino-Japanese compounds, that are overwhelmingly unaccented; 2-mora nouns spread across [0], [1], [2] with atamadaka well represented — and short nouns are exactly what the pitch minimal pairs and earliest vocabulary consist of. State it by length: default [0] for 3+ morae; store accent explicitly for 2-mora nouns, where no default is safe.\n- **Loanword default [-3]** [2 lenses] — fails on the plan's own week-1 corpus: コーヒー is 4 morae, antepenult is the second half of a long vowel, shift-left predicts [1], actual is [3]. A large minority of common loanwords are heiban (パソコン, アメリカ, memo, kamera, garasu, sarada). Keep as a *parsing* fallback for unfamiliar heard words with stated coverage (~70-90%); require dictionary lookup for anything entering the deck.\n- **\"All -masu forms accented on ma, without exception\"** [2 lenses] — true for plain -masu. Scope it: ‑ましょう, ‑ません, ‑ませんでした, ‑ますか have their own patterns and arrive in week 3.\n- **\"Neither fu nor hi has a contrast partner\"** — true in the native stratum, but the loanword series ファ/フィ/フェ/フォ makes [ɸ] contrastive (fan/han, foomu/hoomu), and the plan's own unit-5 corpus includes fooku. The zero-comprehension-cost conclusion survives; the premise as stated does not.\n- **\"Discrimination gains decay silently under vocabulary load\"** — retention studies point the other way (Lively et al. 1994: /r/-/l/ largely retained at 3 months; Wang et al. 1999: Mandarin tone retained at 6 months, both without further training). The re-audits are still worth their 45 minutes as insurance; the rationale should say \"we don't know, so we measure.\"\n- **\"A schwa has no measurable duration\"** — false; schwa is short, not durationless. The real point (a reduced vowel is an unreliable ratio denominator) survives without the hyperbole.\n- **\"A one-mora *des* is wrong\"** — utterance-final /su/ is frequently reduced to near-zero in casual speech and native listeners accept it. The pedagogy is right for a beginner; the absolute claim is not.\n- **\"A learner at ceiling on isolated pairs scores near chance in connected speech\"** — direction right, \"near chance\" exaggerated; the honest version is a substantial but partial drop.\n- **\"Their rhythm never recovers\"** — unfalsifiable and inconsistent with adult phonetic plasticity.\n- **\"Fricative-before-stop-geminate buys half a day\" / \"textbook order costs roughly a day\"** — the acoustic reasoning (positive cue before negative cue) is genuinely good and the ordering is free, so keep it. But English listeners do attend closure duration allophonically, no study has compared the orders, and 2.3 says cross-contrast transfer is weak anyway. Present as a reasoned bet with no measured payoff.\n- **\"Free word segmentation\"** — see 1.10; it is phrase segmentation, and that L2 learners convert a week-1 discrimination drill into an online segmentation cue in continuous speech is a large inferential leap with no supporting study. It is also the payoff justifying the whole pitch investment, so the hedge matters.\n- **\"The learner can independently predict the beat count from the English source, which makes unit 5 self-checking\"** — false for most of the unit's own items, because the transliteration rules (/u/ epenthesis but /o/ after t and d, English /ɚ/→long vowel, short-vowel+stop→geminate, coda /n/→N, and the idiosyncratic sandoicchi) are taught nowhere. A learner who predicts 4 for サンドイッチ and hears 6 cannot tell whether their ear or their prediction failed — worse than no self-check. Either teach the ~8 correspondences in 10 minutes at the head of unit 5, or source beat counts from a kana lookup in the generation script.\n- **\"The /r/ vs /l/ contrast is irrelevant here\"** — largely true for perception; for production, lateral substitution for /r/ is a common audible beginner error with no check anywhere in the plan.\n\n---\n\n## 3. This is why konpyuutaa is [3] and tooru is [1]. The analysis states two invariants and uses this one silently. It is a hard structural constraint the learner can use to eliminate impossible hypotheses, and it makes the shift rules coherent instead of arbitrary.\n\n**3.3 An accent schema that can represent conjugation and enclisis.** A single integer per lexeme cannot represent the copula and particles, whose contour is determined by the host phrase (the `de·shi·ta [0]` error is the symptom), nor accent shift across conjugated forms. The card needs a class label plus a rule reference, not just a number — decided before card #1.\n\n**3.4 A beat-count card type.** The plan says \"the beat count is testable\" and never specifies the test. Audio → type an integer is one of the very few genuinely auto-gradable, ungameable card types available to an audio-first learner with no orthography. It should be the primary card type from item #1 and it is left on the table.\n\n**3.5 Continuous perceptual measurement, and spacing as an explicit design parameter** [2 lenses]. The plan's own thesis is that discrimination decays invisibly, and its response is three audits — a monthly sampling rate for a quantity it says degrades silently. The learner builds their own SRS: interleaving 15-20 discrimination trials into the daily review queue costs 90 seconds, produces a continuous accuracy curve, and makes each audit a confirmation rather than a discovery. Same tooling makes spacing intervals and overnight consolidation schedulable parameters rather than accidents.\n\n**3.6 A held-out human talker set, collected before day 1.** Unit 25 is built on new talkers and correctly notes that re-testing on trained voices measures memory. But a new *voice* from the same engine is not a new talker for duration or accent. The audit needs reserved, never-trained-on human tokens — collected before the block, not at week 4.\n\n**3.7 The moraic N as a nasalized vocoid before vowels, glides and fricatives.** tan'i [taɰ̃i], sen'en [seɰ̃eɴ], hansei [haɰ̃seː]. This is the exact environment of unit 10's two flagship pairs, and it is *why* they are hard — there is no consonantal landmark at all, only a nasalized stretch that must be counted as a beat. Unit 11 covers [m]/[n]/[ŋ]/[ɴ] and omits the one allophone the unit-10 pairs turn on.\n\n**3.8 The no-consecutive-devoicing constraint.** Of two adjacent devoiceable morae, typically only one devoices (kiku, kutsushita, chikatetsu, shitsurei) — usually the first, with dialect and rate variation. Without it, a learner given the rule as stated will over-apply and delete two beats in a row, which is the precise failure unit 18 exists to prevent.\n\n**3.9 Palatalization and affrication as allophony, not as separate consonants.** /s/→[ɕ] before /i/, /t/→[tɕ] before /i/ and [ts] before /u/, /h/→[ç]/[ɸ], /z/→[dʑ]. Unit 17 drills tsu and names fu and hi but never states that shi/chi/tsu are the same phonemes as sa/ta/tu. This becomes structurally load-bearing in week 3 (matsu/machimasu/matte, hanasu/hanashimasu, motsu/mochimasu) — an audio-first learner with no kana has no other way to see these are one paradigm.\n\n**3.10 Connected-speech reduction as a class** [2 lenses]. te-iru→teru, -te shimau→chau, ra-row deletion (wakaranai→wakannai), no→n, de wa→ja, particle dropping, phrase-level compression. These are *timing* changes — they attack the beat grid the whole block installs — and the week-4 shadowing stage runs straight into them unprepared. One lens goes further and argues these dominate real conversational listening failure more than length minimal pairs do; a dimension claiming to cover \"what must be trained before vocabulary\" for a listening-first learner cannot omit the class most responsible for adults failing to parse natural speech.\n\n**3.11 Vowel coalescence generally.** ei→[eː] gets one aside, but the same applies to ou→[oː] (why arigatou is arigatoo — assumed by the notation, never explained), casual ai→[eː] (sugee, wakannee), and long vowels arising across morpheme boundaries. For a learner with no orthography this is the only account they will ever get of why long vowels exist.\n\n**3.12 Intervocalic /g/ → [ŋ].** Ranked tenth and then never mentioned again. For a listener with no orthography, hearing [kaŋakɯ] and [kaɡakɯ] as the same morpheme is a *recognition* problem, and it is generational — so it will be inconsistent across the six voices the plan mandates. Give it the same \"listening only, no drill\" treatment as N-assimilation.\n\n**3.13 An aptitude/variability branch and a non-responder plan.** The pretest currently \"does not remove any unit\" and only reweights time, so the one branch that matters — gradual vs immediate variability escalation — is never taken (see 2.6). Praat/parselmouth gives F1-F2 trajectories (offglide detection, unit 3), F0 and intensity tracks (unit 4), and DTW alignment against a model token (unit 2).
