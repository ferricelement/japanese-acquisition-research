# Collocation listening scripts: Stage 2

Listening scripts for the collocations list (21-list-collocations.json), one per chunk, in course order. Each script is a short scene where that chunk's collocations come up several times, plus a few from earlier chunks for review. For each one: make the audio from the Japanese (one voice per speaker, the same voices in every script; if a voice misreads a kanji, use the kana version of that line), listen without looking at any text, answer the questions out loud, then check the answers and the romaji transcript. Say-it questions (type respond) have no choices: a character says the cue line and you answer in Japanese. Words outside the versatility list (up to the script's stage) and the collocations so far are listed as new words.

## 23. Just one cup

<summary>Answers and transcript</summary>

1. **A**. Lines 2-4: Kenta is making dinner (gohan, mou sugu dekiru yo; kenta ga gohan tsukutte kureteru no?). Line 5: Sora wants a bath first, and in line 10, thirty minutes later, the food has gone cold (gohan, samechatta yo). Line 13: Sora admits he already ate before coming home (mou gohan tabete kichatta), but in lines 15 and 17 he eats anyway (kenta no gohan wa taberu yo! ... samete mo oishii!). B swaps the two: Kenta is the one cooking and Sora is the one in the bath. C is wrong: Sora doesn't go to bed. He sits down and eats Kenta's dinner even though he's full (line 17: un, ippai. demo...).
2. Say not yet, using mada and the negative of taberu's -teru form: uun, mada gohan tabetenai. Don't say tabenakatta: that means you skipped the meal. For example: *uun, mada gohan tabetenai.* (No, I haven't eaten yet.); *uun, gohan mada tabetenai n da. onaka suita yo.* (No, I haven't had dinner yet. I'm starving.). gohan is any meal, not only rice: in this scene Kenta's gohan is hamburger steak (line 4). For 'not yet', Japanese uses mada with the negative -teru form, exactly as Kenta says in line 4 (boku mo mada gohan tabetenai n da). tabenakatta would mean you didn't eat the meal at all, not that you haven't got to it yet. If you had already eaten, you'd say un, tabeta, or, like Sora in line 13, mou gohan tabete kichatta (I already ate before I got here).
3. **C**. gohan tsukuru means 'cook a meal, make dinner': tomorrow Sora cooks, the way Kenta did tonight (line 14: gohan tsukutta noni, 'I made dinner!'). A is the rice trap: gohan here is the whole meal, and tonight's was hamburger steak. Cooking just the rice would be gohan o taku. B: Kenta says nothing about Sora coming home. His jab comes in the next sentence: gohan dekitara, boku wa sugu iku kara (the second dinner's ready, I'll be right there), unlike Sora and his thirty-minute bath.
4. **B**. Line 8: sora! gohan dekita yo! The kitchen call uses dekiru in the past: the meal has been made, so it's ready. A is what you get by translating 'ready' with junbi. Japanese would say gohan no junbi ga dekita, which still uses dekiru, and the everyday call is simply gohan dekita yo. C, gohan owatta, means the meal is over and everyone has finished eating, which is the opposite moment. Notice the tense: in line 2 Kenta said mou sugu dekiru yo. Non-past dekiru means it'll be ready very soon, not that it's ready now.
5. Warn him using the -chau form of sameru: e, ima? gohan samechau yo. Not samuku naru: samui is for the weather and for how a person feels. For example: *e, ima? gohan samechau yo.* (What, now? The food'll get cold.); *gohan samechau kara, tabete kara ni shite yo.* (The food'll get cold, so have your bath after you eat.). When hot food loses its heat, the verb is sameru. samechau adds 'and that would be a shame', which is why it's the usual warning. Kenta says it in line 6, and half an hour later he says it in the past in line 10 (gohan, samechatta yo). In the same scene Sora says samukatta and samukute (lines 1 and 5). That's samui, used for the cold weather outside and for how he feels, never for food. In line 17 Sora even eats the food cold: samete mo oishii (it's good even cold).

<summary>Answers and transcript</summary>

1. **C**. Line 2: Kenji's fever came down yesterday (kusuri nondara, sugu sagatta). Line 4: he says his cold isn't better yet (mada naottenai) and asks to skip school again. Lines 8-13: he wasn't sleeping. He was lying down playing games, so of course he's still tired. Line 15: Yuki will keep the game until his cold is better (kaze ga naoru made). In line 16 it's suddenly better (ima naotta!), so in lines 17-18 he agrees to go to school. A is wrong: his fever is down (lines 2 and 5). Yuki only says she'll keep the game until he's better. B is wrong: Yuki never lets him stay home. She uses the gaming to show he isn't really that sick.
2. **A**. Line 7: Yuki assumes he slept all day (kyou zutto neteta noni?). Line 8: Kenji corrects her with nete wa nai yo (I wasn't asleep) and zutto yoko ni natteta dake (I was just lying down). Yoko ni naru means lying down to rest, not neru (sleeping). Yuki picks up on it in line 9 (yoko ni natte, nani shiteta no?), and it turns out he was lying there playing his game (line 10). B takes yoko to mean 'side', but yoko ni naru just means lying down, whichever way you face. C is what Yuki assumed, and Kenji says it isn't true.
3. Say your fever has come down, using sagaru in the past: un, netsu sagatta yo. For example: *un, netsu sagatta yo.* (Yeah, my fever's come down.); *un, kusuri nondara, netsu sagatta.* (Yeah, I took some medicine and my fever went down.). A fever comes down by itself with sagaru, so netsu takes ga, which casual speech often drops: netsu sagatta. Yuki asks it that way in line 1, and Kenji answers in line 2 (un, sagatta). Don't use oriru or heru here. Netsu o sageru (with o) means someone bringing the fever down. That's why Kenji puts the medicine in a separate clause: kusuri nondara, sugu sagatta. Yuki only said 'netsu wa?', so you have to supply the verb yourself.
4. Say it's better now, using naoru in the past with mou: un, mou naotta yo. For example: *un, mou naotta yo.* (Yeah, it's all better now.); *un, kaze mou naotta. arigatou.* (Yeah, I'm over my cold. Thanks.). A cold clears up with naoru. Its past form is naotta, and adding mou makes it 'better now'. Kenji says exactly that in line 16 (a, naotta! ima naotta!) once his game is at stake. Mada naottenai (lines 4 and 14) is the 'not yet' answer. Kaze takes ga, which is often dropped. Kaze o naosu (with o) means someone doing the healing, as in hayaku naoshite ne (get well soon).
5. **B**. Line 6: tsukare ga mada torenai. Tiredness 'comes off' you (toreru), the way a stain or a button comes off. Yuki repeats it in line 7 and again in line 13 (tsukare mo torenai yo). A is the trap: naoru is for getting over an illness or injury (kaze ga naoru, lines 3 and 16), and tiredness isn't an illness that gets cured. C borrows sagaru from the fever (netsu wa sagatta, line 5). Sagaru is for things that go down on a scale, like a fever, a temperature or a price. Tiredness doesn't go down. It comes off.
