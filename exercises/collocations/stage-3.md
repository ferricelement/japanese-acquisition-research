# Collocation listening scripts: Stage 3

Listening scripts for the collocations list (21-list-collocations.json), one per chunk, in course order. Each script is a short scene where that chunk's collocations come up several times, plus a few from earlier chunks for review. For each one: make the audio from the Japanese (one voice per speaker, the same voices in every script; if a voice misreads a kanji, use the kana version of that line), listen without looking at any text, answer the questions out loud, then check the answers and the romaji transcript. Say-it questions (type respond) have no choices: a character says the cue line and you answer in Japanese. Words outside the versatility list (up to the script's stage) and the collocations so far are listed as new words.

## 47. Coffee on Satou-san

*Costs and paying · Polite dialogue · 18 lines · Yuki, Satou-san*

A Saturday afternoon in an old coffee shop near the station. Yuki ran into her elderly neighbour Satou-san on the shopping street and helped carry his bags, and he took her for a coffee at the place he has been going to for years. Over coffee he has some news, and when it is time to leave he has a surprise for her.

**Collocations:** *okane ga kakaru* (cost money); *ikura suru* (how much it costs (price + suru)); *nedan ga takai* (the price is high); *okane o harau* (pay (money)); *warikan ni suru* (split the bill).

**Review:** *igokochi ga ii* (comfortable to be in, cosy); *piano o narau* (take piano lessons); *toire ni iku* (go to the toilet).

### Questions

1. **What happens in this conversation?**  
   nani ga arimashita ka? (何がありましたか？)
   - A. Over coffee, Satou-san tells Yuki about his piano lessons, and it turns out he has already paid for both of them.
   - B. Satou-san tells Yuki about the new piano he bought, and they split the bill for their coffee.
   - C. Yuki treats Satou-san to coffee, but he insists on splitting the bill.

2. **Kenta says he's thinking of buying a car. Warn him that having a car costs quite a lot.** *(say it)*  
   kuruma, kaou kana. (車、買おうかな。)

3. **After hearing what the lessons cost, Yuki asks about the price of a piano. What did she say?**  
   yuki-san wa, piano ni tsuite nan to kikimashita ka? (ユキさんは、ピアノについて何と聞きましたか？)
   - A. *piano wa, ikura kosuto shimasu ka?* (How much does a piano cost? (word for word))
   - B. *piano tte, ikura kakaru n desu ka?* (How much does piano cost you, all in?)
   - C. *piano tte, ikura suru n desu ka?* (How much is a piano?)

4. **After tasting the coffee, what does Yuki say about it?**  
   yuki-san wa, koohii o nonde, nan to iimashita ka? (ユキさんは、コーヒーを飲んで、何と言いましたか？)
   - A. It's cheaper than she expected, so she'll come again.
   - B. It's a bit pricey, but it's so good she'd come back anyway.
   - C. It's good, but too pricey for her to come back.

5. **You're at a café with Kenta. While he was in the toilet, you paid the bill. Now he says "Right, shall we head off?" and gets out his wallet. Tell him you've already paid.** *(say it)*  
   jaa, sorosoro kaerou ka. (じゃあ、そろそろ帰ろうか。)

6. **You've had dinner with Kenta. He looks at the bill and says it comes to four thousand yen altogether. Suggest splitting it evenly.** *(say it)*

- **toki** (とき): when, at the time (toire ni itta toki ni = when I went to the toilet)

<summary>Answers and transcript</summary>

1. **A**. Line 6: Satou-san says he has been taking piano lessons lately. In line 13 Yuki suggests splitting the bill (kyou wa warikan ni shimashou ne), but in line 14 he says he has already paid, when he went to the toilet. B is wrong twice: the piano isn't new, it's the one his children played years ago (line 10), and they don't split the bill. C swaps who did what: Yuki is the one who wants to split, and Satou-san has paid for both of them.
2. Warn him about the overall cost with okane ga kakaru, casually since it's Kenta (the ga often drops): kuruma tte kekkou okane kakaru yo. For example: *e, kuruma tte kekkou okane kakaru yo.* (Hm, cars cost quite a bit, you know.); *ii kedo, kuruma motsu to okane kakaru yo.* (Sure, but having a car costs money.). Having a car costs money overall (petrol, parking, repairs), so the verb is kakaru, as when Yuki asks whether piano costs a lot (line 7: kekkou okane kakarimasen ka?) and Satou-san answers okane wa kakarimasu (line 8). The car's price tag on its own would take suru: kuruma tte ikura suru no? (compare lines 9-10). Japanese has no single verb for 'cost', and kosuto suru isn't said.
3. **C**. Line 9: ichiman en! piano tte, ikura suru n desu ka? A single thing's price takes suru, and Satou-san answers in the same frame: gojuuman en mo shimasu (line 10). B uses the verb for overall spending: kakaru is what Yuki used for the lessons in line 7 (okane kakarimasen ka?), the running cost of doing piano, not what one piano goes for. A is English 'cost' word for word. kosuto is a business word for expenses, and Japanese never uses it as a verb for a price.
4. **B**. Line 5: kore nara, nedan ga chotto takakute mo, mata kitai desu. nedan ga takai means the price is high, and -te mo means 'even if', so the price doesn't put her off. Satou-san had just said nedan wa chotto takai kedo, oishii n desu yo (line 4). A gets the price backwards: the coffee is roppyaku en mo (a whole 600 yen, line 3), and a low price would be yasui. C catches takai but misses -te mo ... mata kitai: she does want to come again.
5. Say it's done, with harau in the past: okane wa mou haratta yo. Keep it casual, since it's Kenta. For example: *a, okane wa mou haratta yo.* (Oh, I've already paid.); *daijoubu, okane wa sakki haratta kara.* (It's fine, I paid earlier.). Handing money over is harau, and the past haratta says it's done, just as Satou-san tells Yuki in line 14 (okane wa mou haraimashita yo, sakki, toire ni itta toki ni). Don't say kaeshita: kaesu is paying back money you borrowed. Offering to pay is more often dasu (koko wa watashi ga dasu yo), and treating someone is ogoru.
6. Suggest it with warikan ni shiyou, and add each person's share if you like: hitori nisen en ne. For example: *jaa, warikan ni shiyou. hitori nisen en ne.* (OK, let's split it. Two thousand each.); *kyou wa warikan ni shiyou ka.* (Shall we split it today?). Splitting a bill evenly is one noun, warikan, with ni suru, as in Yuki's kyou wa warikan ni shimashou ne (line 13) and tsugi wa zettai, warikan ni shimashou ne! (line 17). English 'split' leads to wakeru, which is for asking staff for separate bills (kaikei wakete moraemasu ka?). Each person paying for their own is betsubetsu.

## 48. The wrong trousers

*Trying clothes on · Casual dialogue · 18 lines · Sora, Mai*

A Saturday afternoon in a clothes shop. Sora starts a new part-time job at a restaurant next week, and Mai has come along to help him find a pair of black trousers. She pulls a pair off a rail and he takes them into the fitting room, chatting with her through the curtain.

**Collocations:** *zubon o haku* (put on / wear trousers); *fuku o kigaeru* (change clothes, get changed); *fuku ga niau* (suit someone, look good on someone); *saizu ga au* (be the right size, fit).

**Review:** *fuku o kiru* (put on / wear clothes); *sutairu ga ii* (have a good figure); *ki ni iru* (like, be pleased with).

### Questions

<details>
<summary>Answers and transcript</summary>

1. **B**. Line 7: Mai realises they're in the women's section (koko, rediisu da), so the pair she gave Sora was women's, and that's why he couldn't get them on (line 6: saizu awanai, hairanai; line 8: saizu awanai wake da). In line 9 she brings men's trousers, they fit (line 10), and he takes them (line 12: kore ni suru). Then Mai tries on the women's pair and buys it (line 17: kore kau), and Sora jokes that they came to buy his trousers (line 18). A is wrong: the first pair was his usual size, and the problem was that it was women's; Mai brought men's trousers, not a bigger size. A also leaves out Mai buying a pair. C is wrong: in line 2 Sora says the restaurant gives him the top, but the trousers have to be his own.
2. **C**. Line 2: 'zubon wa jibun no o haku n da'. Trousers take haku, the same verb as shoes, because you step into them. In the same line Sora uses kiru for the top (ue wa mise no fuku o kiru), so you hear the two verbs side by side. Mai uses haku too: 'kuroi zubon o haku' in line 1 and 'haite mite' (try them on) in line 9. A is the English speaker's trap: kiru is for things on the upper body, like shirts and jackets, not trousers. B is wrong: tsukeru is for accessories such as a necklace or a watch, not trousers.
3. Say you'll go and change with kigaeru, usually as kigaete kuru (go, get changed and come back). fuku can stay or be dropped, and you can ask him to wait with chotto matte. For example: *chotto matte. fuku kigaete kuru ne.* (Hang on. I'll just go and get changed.); *ii yo. demo saki ni kigaete kuru kara, chotto mattete.* (Sure. But I'll get changed first, so wait a sec.). Sora says 'fuku kigaete kuru ne' in line 12, and Mai says 'watashi mo chotto kigaete kuru' in line 15: the -te kuru form means you go, do it, and come back. Mai's 'kigaeta?' in line 5 (are you changed?) shows that fuku is often left out, because kigaeru already means changing clothes. Don't say fuku o kaeru: that's about switching to a different outfit, not getting changed. Sora is a friend speaking casually, so answer casually.
4. Say it suits her with niau. In casual speech ga drops and so does the noun: niau yo, or yoku niatteru (it really suits you). sugoku adds emphasis. For example: *un, sugoku niau yo!* (Yeah, it really suits you!); *ii ne! yoku niatteru yo.* (Nice! It looks great on you.). In line 11 Mai tells Sora 'niau niau!', and in line 16 Sora tells Mai 'hora, niau jan!' when she comes out in the women's trousers. niau is about how something looks on a person. Whether it's the right size is a different phrase, saizu ga au: in line 10 Sora checks both, one after the other (saizu atteru. dou? niau?). A shop assistant, or anyone speaking politely, says 'yoku oniai desu ne'. Mai asks casually, so answer casually.
5. **A**. Line 10: 'a, kore wa saizu atteru'. With saizu as the subject, au (here atteru) means the size is right: unlike the women's pair, which wasn't his size and wouldn't go on (line 6: saizu awanai, hairanai), these fit. B is wrong: 'looks good on me' is niau, which Sora asks about straight after, as a separate question (dou? niau?). C is wrong: au can mean 'go with' when one thing matches another, but Sora is talking about the size, not about what the trousers go with.

From now on I'll let you know.); *gomen ne, shinpai kakechatte. sumaho, uchi ni wasurechatta n da.* (Sorry for making you worry. I'd left my phone at home.). Mom says shinpai shita (line 12) and Yuki says okaasan, zettai shinpai shiteru yo (line 9), because Mom is the one worrying. When you're the cause, you put the worry on her with kakeru. 'shinpai shite gomen' would mean you were the one worrying, which makes no sense here. She's your mother, so casual speech fits. With someone like Tanaka-san you'd say shinpai o kakete sumimasen.
5. Tell him to cheer up: genki dashite (the o drops in casual speech). You can soften it with daijoubu da yo, or add that there's always next time. Don't lead with ganbatte, which tells him to keep trying when what he needs is to feel better. For example: *daijoubu da yo, genki dashite. tsugi ga aru jan.* (It's okay, cheer up. There's always next time.); *sokka. maa, genki dashite. nanka oishii mono demo tabe ni iku?* (I see. Well, cheer up. Want to go get something nice to eat?). Mom (line 14) and Yuki (line 18) both say genki dashite to Kenji while he's down. Line 14 also lets you hear the contrast. tsugi ganbareba ii (you can just try harder next time) is about effort later, while genki dashite is about feeling better now. ganbatte on its own can feel like pressure to someone who's already low, and genki ni natte is what you say to someone who's ill (get well). Kenji is family, so casual speech fits.

## 69. The wrong sunrise

*Sun and moon · Casual dialogue · 14 lines · Kenji, Okaasan*

A Saturday in autumn, around five in the afternoon. Kenji, Yuki's little brother, gamed through the night and slept all day. He shuffles into the living room, where his mother is, as the low sun shines in through the window.

**Collocations:** *hi ga kureru* (it gets dark (evening)); *hi ga noboru* (the sun rises, comes up); *hi ga shizumu* (the sun sets, goes down); *tsuki ga deru* (the moon comes out).

<summary>Answers and transcript</summary>

1. **B**. Lines 1-3: Kenji wakes up, sees the low sun and says mou hi ga nobotteru! okureru!, thinking it's morning. His mother tells him it's five and mou sugu hi ga kureru (it'll be dark soon), and he realises the sun isn't coming up but going down (shizundeku tokoro). Lines 4-5: he gamed until the sun came up (ki ga tsuitara hi ga nobotteta) and then slept all day. A is wrong: he never meant to watch a sunrise; the 'sunrise' he saw was the sunset. C is wrong: okureru! was only his sleepy panic. It's Saturday (line 7) and five in the afternoon.
2. Agree politely (he's your elderly neighbour) and use hi ga kureru: sou desu ne, hi ga kureru no ga hayaku narimashita ne. For example: *sou desu ne. hi ga kureru no ga hayaku narimashita ne.* (It's true. It gets dark so much earlier now, doesn't it?); *hontou desu ne. saikin wa hi ga kureru no ga hayai desu ne.* (You're right. It gets dark early these days, doesn't it?). The evenings drawing in is hi ga kureru, the classic autumn remark: Okaasan says saikin wa hi ga kureru no ga hayai (line 6) and mou sugu hi ga kureru yo (line 2). Kureru here is the day closing, not kureru 'give me'. Kuraku naru no ga hayaku narimashita ne (it gets dark earlier) says the same thing, and people also say hi ga ochiru no ga hayai. Hi ga shizumu is the sun itself going down at a given moment (line 7: a, hi ga shizunjatta), not the evening coming on. Use -masu forms with Satou-san.
3. **C**. Line 1: mou hi ga nobotteru! He says it again in line 5 (ki ga tsuitara hi ga nobotteta), and his mother uses it in line 12 (hi ga noboru made). Nobotteru means the sun is up now, not that it is in the middle of rising. A: agaru is the everyday 'go up' (stairs, prices), and English 'rise' points to it, but it isn't the usual verb for the sun: the sun noboru. B: okiteru is 'up' as in awake. People and pets okiru; the sun doesn't.
4. Suggest going once the sun is down, with hi ga shizumu in the -tara or -te kara form: hi ga shizundara ikou. For example: *uun, hi ga shizundara ikou yo.* (Nah, let's go once the sun's down.); *mada atsui kara, hi ga shizunde kara ni shiyou.* (It's still hot, so let's wait until the sun's gone down.). The sun going below the horizon is hi ga shizumu, the same verb as a ship sinking: Kenji says a, hi ga shizunjatta (line 7) and hi ga shizunde, tsuki ga dete (line 11). Not hi ga oriru or hi ga sagaru: English 'go down' points there, but the sun sinks. Hi ga ochiru is also natural in chat. Hi ga kuretara ikou would also be fine Japanese, but it means after dark, a little later: in lines 7-8 the sun goes down first (shizunjatta) and only then does it get dark (hi ga kurete kita).
5. **A**. Line 13: tsuki ga deteru aida dake ni suru. Deteru means the moon is out, a state that lasts, not the moment it appears, so 'only while the moon's out'. Kenji first spots it in line 9 (atchi, tsuki ga deteru), just after sunset, and line 10 says it's a full moon (mangetsu). A full moon is up from sunset until morning, which is why his mother says sore, asa made desho! (line 14). B reads deteru as 'comes out'. That would be tsuki ga detara yameru, and the moon was already out. C: nobody suggests going outside.
