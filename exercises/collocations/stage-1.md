# Collocation listening scripts: Stage 1

Listening scripts for the collocations list (21-list-collocations.json), one per chunk, in course order. Each script is a short scene where that chunk's collocations come up several times, plus a few from earlier chunks for review. For each one: make the audio from the Japanese (one voice per speaker, the same voices in every script; if a voice misreads a kanji, use the kana version of that line), listen without looking at any text, answer the questions out loud, then check the answers and the romaji transcript. Say-it questions (type respond) have no choices: a character says the cue line and you answer in Japanese. Words outside the versatility list (up to the script's stage) and the collocations so far are listed as new words.

## 1. Umbrella up this time

*Rain and snow · Casual dialogue · 18 lines · Yuki, Kenta*

A winter evening. Yuki turns up at Kenta's flat dripping wet and waits there for the rain to stop.

**Collocations:** *ame ga furu* (it rains; rain falls); *yuki ga furu* (it snows); *ame ga yamu* (the rain stops); *kasa o sasu* (put up (open) an umbrella; have an umbrella up); *ame ni furareru* (get caught in the rain).

### Questions

1. **What happens in this scene?**  
   nani ga arimashita ka? (何がありましたか？)
   - A. Yuki forgot her umbrella and waits at Kenta's until the snow stops.
   - B. Yuki got rained on even though she had her umbrella with her; now it's snowing, and this time she'll put it up.
   - C. Kenta got rained on today, so Yuki lends him her umbrella before she goes.

2. **Kenta is about to head out and asks you, "ima kara deru n da kedo, soto, ame?" (I'm heading out. Is it raining?) It is. Tell him it's raining.** *(say it)*

3. **After the rain stops, Kenta looks out and says "soto! yuki futteru yo!" What is happening outside?**  
   ima, soto wa? (今、外は？)
   - A. The snow has just stopped, like the rain did.
   - B. There's snow on the ground from earlier, but nothing is falling.
   - C. It has started snowing, and the snow is coming down right now.

4. **Yuki asks you, "ne, soto mada ame futteru?" (Is it still raining out?) You look out: the rain has stopped. Tell her.** *(say it)*  
   ne, soto mada ame futteru? (ね、外まだ雨降ってる？)

5. **Kenta asks Yuki a 'why' question about her umbrella. What did he ask?**  
   kenta-san wa nan to iimashita ka? (ケンタさんは何と言いましたか？)

   - B. *jaa, nande kasa akenakatta no?* (So why didn't you open your umbrella?)
   - C. *jaa, nande kasa mottenakatta no?* (So why didn't you have your umbrella with you?)

6. **You turn up at Kenta's flat soaking wet. He asks, "e, daijoubu? nani ga atta no?" (Whoa, are you okay? What happened?) Tell him you got caught in the rain.** *(say it)*

- **shika** (しか): only (with a negative: chotto shika futtenakatta = it was barely raining)
- **kyuu ni** (急に): suddenly, all of a sudden
- **made** (まで): until (ame yamu made = until the rain stops)
- **soto** (外): outside
- **koto** (こと): thing, matter; X no koto = about X (yuki no koto ja nakute = I don't mean you)
- **kondo** (今度): this time; next time
- **akeru** (開ける): open (a door, a box)
- **iu** (言う): say

Lines 4-5: she had her umbrella (motteru yo, kore) but didn't put it up (nande kasa sasanakatta no?), because at first it was barely raining (line 6: chotto shika futtenakatta). Line 13: the rain has stopped (mou yanda yo), and lines 15-16: it's snowing. Lines 17-18: this time she'll put it up (kondo wa kasa sasu? / sasu yo!). A is wrong: she had her umbrella all along, and she waited for the rain to stop, not the snow (line 10). C is wrong: Kenta got rained on yesterday (line 9: kinou), and Yuki keeps her umbrella.
2. Say it's raining with ame and furu in its -teru form: un, ame futteru yo. For example: *un, ame futteru yo.* (Yeah, it's raining.); *un, ame futte kita yo.* (Yeah, it's started raining.). Rain falls with furu, not ochiru. Futteru is 'it's raining right now', as in lines 3 and 12 (ame futteru no? / soto mada ame futteru?). Futte kita is 'it's started raining', the shape Yuki uses in line 6 (kyuu ni metcha futte kite). Kenta only said 'ame?', so the verb has to come from you.
3. **C**. Line 15: yuki futteru is snow falling right now: the same furu as rain, in its -teru form. Yuki looks and agrees in line 16 (honto ni yuki futteru!). A is wrong: mou yanda in line 13 was the rain stopping; for the snow Kenta says futteru, falling right now (lines 15-16). B would be yuki tsumotteru (the snow has settled on the ground), not futteru. In line 13 Kenta's 'a, yuki!' means snow, but Yuki thinks he is calling her name (line 14: e, nani?), so in line 15 he says yuki no koto ja nakute (I don't mean you).
4. Say no, it has stopped, with yamu in the past: uun, ame mou yanda yo. For example: *uun, ame mou yanda yo.* (No, the rain's stopped.); *iie, ame wa mou yamimashita yo.* (No, the rain has stopped now.). Rain stops by itself, so it takes yamu: yanda (casual past), yamimashita (polite past). Not tomaru, and not yameru, which is a person quitting something. Kenta answers the same way in line 13, with ame left out (uun, mou yanda yo). The second model is the polite version, for someone like Tanaka-san or Satou-san.
5. **A**. Line 5: kasa o sasu is holding an umbrella up over you, and Kenta asks why she didn't (sasanakatta). B is the English word 'open' put into Japanese: you don't akeru an umbrella, you sasu it (hiraku is only the moment it pops open). C uses motsu, carrying it, and Yuki had just shown him she did have it (line 4: motteru yo, kore). Her problem was having it and not putting it up, which Kenta teases her about again in line 7 and line 17.
6. Say the rain fell on you, with furareru (the passive of furu): ame ni furarechatta. For example: *ame ni furarechatta. kyuu ni futte kite.* (I got caught in the rain. It came on all of a sudden.); *ame ni furarechaimashita.* (I got caught in the rain.). English 'caught' points to tsukamaru, but Japanese says the rain fell on you: ame ni furareta, and -chatta adds 'and that was bad luck'. That's Yuki's line 2 (ame ni furarechatta), and Kenta uses it in lines 7 and 9. Plain ame ga futta would only report the weather; furareta says it happened to you. The second model is the polite version, for someone like Satou-san.

## 2. Cake before curry

*Hungry, full, thirsty · Casual dialogue · 18 lines · Kenta, Sora*

1. **What happens in this scene?**

- **karee** (カレー): curry (Japanese curry, usually with rice)
- **tsukuru** (作る): make, cook (karee tsukutteru = I'm making curry)
- **mizu** (水): water
- **keeki** (ケーキ): cake
- **taberu** (食べる): eat
- **ippai** (一杯): one glass, one cupful (mizu, mou ippai = another glass of water); a different word from the ippai in onaka ippai

<summary>Answers and transcript</summary>

1. **B**. Line 2: Sora gets home starving (onaka hetta). Lines 7-8: the curry needs thirty more minutes, so he decides to eat the cakes he brought home. Line 9: Kenta warns him he'll fill up (onaka ippai ni natchau yo), and in line 10 Sora says he's far too hungry for that. Lines 11-13: when the curry is ready he's full (mou onaka ippai), because he ate all three cakes. Line 18: he'll have the curry tomorrow. A is wrong: Kenta does say no (line 9), but Sora eats the cakes anyway, and what waits for tomorrow is the curry. C is wrong: he came home hungry (line 2); what filled him up was the three cakes at home (line 13), not food at work.
2. Say yes and that you're hungry, with suku in the past: un, taberu! onaka suita. For example: *un, taberu! onaka suita.* (Yes, please! I'm hungry.); *honto ni? taberu taberu! metcha onaka suiteta n da.* (Really? Yes, yes! I've been starving.). There's no everyday 'hungry' adjective: your stomach has emptied, onaka suita, in the past tense for hunger you feel now. That's Kenta's line 3 (boku mo onaka suita). The dictionary form onaka suku would sound like 'I get hungry' in general. Suiteta n da, as in the second model and Sora's line 14 (onaka suiteta n da yo), is 'I've been hungry (that's why)'. Sora's onaka hetta (line 2) means the same and is fine with a friend, but suita is the safe default with anyone.
3. **C**. Line 12: mou onaka ippai, 'my stomach is already full', with onaka kept in. B is English 'I am full' word for word: Japanese says the stomach is full, not the person, and boku wa ippai da doesn't mean that. A drops onaka, and mou ippai on its own sounds like 'one more (glass)': exactly what Sora asks for in line 16 (mizu, mou ippai ii?), which is why Kenta teases him in line 17 (onaka ippai na noni, mizu wa mou ippai?).
4. Say your throat has gone dry, with kawaku in the past, then ask for water: un, demo nodo kawaita. mizu aru? For example: *un, demo nodo kawaita. mizu aru?* (Yeah, but I'm thirsty. Is there any water?); *daijoubu, daijoubu. demo metcha nodo kawaita. mizu aru?* (I'm fine, I'm fine. But I'm really thirsty. Got any water?). There's no 'thirsty' adjective either: the throat (nodo) has dried (kawaku), and like suita it goes in the past for thirst you feel now: nodo kawaita, just as Sora says in line 4 (ato, nodo mo kawaita. mizu aru?). In line 16 he uses the -chau form, kawaichatta, for 'the cake's gone and made me thirsty'. Plain nodo kawaku would sound like 'I get thirsty' in general, and nodo ga itai is a sore throat, not thirst.
5. **A**. Line 10: onaka hetteru is the -teru state of heru, 'my stomach has emptied', so he's hungry right now, and metcha makes it 'seriously starving': he's sure there's room for cake and curry. Lines 12-13 prove him wrong. B takes heru in its other sense, 'go down', as in taijuu ga heru (lose weight), but after onaka it is hunger. C reads it as the future, which would be plain onaka heru ('I'll get hungry'); -teru is how he feels now, and he eats three cakes, not a little (line 13). Heru is the blunter, casual twin of suku: Sora also says onaka hetta in line 2, and onaka suiteta in line 14 means the same.

The bath is free, which is why their mother offers it to Yuki in line 3. Kenji's own line 17, chotto toire itte kuru, is the other shape: 'I'm just popping to the toilet (and I'll be back)'.
3. Say you're fine, then use shawaa with abiru, ideally in the -te kuru shape: un, daijoubu. chotto shawaa abite kuru ne. For example: *un, daijoubu. chotto shawaa abite kuru ne.* (Yeah, I'm fine. I'll just go and take a shower.); *un, daijoubu. shawaa abite mo ii?* (Yeah, I'm okay. Can I take a shower?). A shower is water poured over you, so the verb is abiru. abite kuru means 'go and shower, then come back', the shape Kenji uses in line 5 (boku ga saki ni shawaa abite kuru). English 'take a shower' points to toru, but nobody says shawaa o toru. ofuro ni hairu is wrong here too: that's getting into a bath, and Kenta would have to fill one first. The second answer uses -te mo ii? to ask permission, which suits a shower in someone else's flat. Casual speech drops o, as both answers do.
4. Use ofuro with hairu, because a bath is something you get into: watashi! saki ni ofuro haitte kuru ne. For example: *watashi! saki ni ofuro haitte kuru ne.* (Me! I'll go and have my bath first.); *jaa, watashi ga saki ni ofuro hairu ne.* (Okay, I'll get in the bath first, then.). You go into a bath, so it's ofuro (ni) hairu, and casual speech usually drops ni. This is Yuki's line 4 (saki ni ofuro haitte kuru ne). Kenji answers it in line 5 with the same frame and the other verb: saki ni shawaa abite kuru. A shower is poured over you (abiru) and a bath is gone into (hairu), so ofuro o abiru and ofuro o toru are both wrong. Their mother uses the same verb in line 18: ofuro haitchatte (go on, get in the bath).
5. Use ha with migaku, in the -te kuru shape: chotto ha migaite kuru. For example: *chotto ha migaite kuru.* (Just going to brush my teeth.); *chotto ha o migaite kimasu.* (I'm just going to brush my teeth.). Teeth are polished, so the verb is migaku. Kenji uses it in lines 13 and 16 (ha migaite, ha mo zutto migaiteta). English speakers often say ha o arau ('wash'), but that's for hands and faces (te o arau, kao o arau). Teeth get migaku. With -te kuru you're saying you'll be right back. Lots of people in Japan brush their teeth at work after lunch. The second answer is the polite version, for Tanaka-san.
6. **A**. Line 13: hige sotte. soru means shaving hair off with a razor, and in line 14 Yuki repeats it to tease him (hige sotta no? doko ni hige aru no?). B is what English 'cut' leads to, but hige o kiru means trimming a beard with scissors, not shaving it off. C is the dictionary trap: kezuru is the 'shave' of shaving wood or a pencil down, and it's never used for hair. Stubble and beards are always soru.

English 'was postponed' tempts the passive enki sareta, which sounds like an official notice; in conversation it's enki ni natta.

<summary>Answers and transcript</summary>

1. **B**. Line 1: Kenta wins a book in the draw, and line 3 says it's a cookbook. Sora only wins an umbrella (lines 5-6), and they already have five at home (line 8), so he feels unlucky (line 10: boku wa honto ni un warui naa). Then it starts raining and Kenta is worried the book will be ruined (line 11). Sora puts up his umbrella and Kenta gets under it (lines 12-13). Sora decides he's the lucky one after all (line 14), and Kenta says he'll cook for him from the book (line 15). A is wrong: the book never gets ruined, because the umbrella keeps it dry. Sora also doesn't cook (line 4: ryouri shinai). C is wrong: Kenta won the cookbook and Sora won the umbrella, and nobody swaps prizes.
2. Tell him he was lucky with luck as the subject: un ii ne! (ga dropped). Or, since it's already happened, use the past: un ga yokatta ne. For example: *e, un ii ne!* (Oh, lucky you!); *sore wa un ga yokatta ne.* (That was lucky.). Sora says the first answer to Kenta in line 2 (e, honto ni? un ii ne!) when Kenta wins the book. In line 14 he keeps ga: honto ni un ga ii no wa boku da ne. In Japanese the luck is the subject (un ga ii: the luck is good), so you don't put 'lucky' on the person the way English does. The past of ii is yokatta, so 'that was lucky' is un ga yokatta, never un iikatta. 'Good luck!' before an exam is ganbatte and has nothing to do with un. Here the exam is already over, so you're commenting on luck that has already happened.
3. **C**. Line 9: un warukatta ne is the usual way to console someone. The luck was bad (warukatta is the past of warui), and ga is dropped. On its own, warui can mean poor quality or at fault, but with un it is only about luck. A is wrong: prizes are won in a draw, not chosen (atatta, lines 1 and 5), and Kenta isn't blaming Sora. B is wrong: Kenta says nothing against the umbrella, and it works fine when the rain comes (lines 12-13). Sora answers in line 10 with both phrases in one frame: kenta wa un ii yo ne. boku wa honto ni un warui naa.
4. **A**. Line 13: yaku ni tatta, the past of yaku ni tatsu, says the umbrella actually helped: it kept Kenta's new book out of the rain. B is what you get by translating the English word 'useful' as benri. Benri means handy or convenient to use, like a shop close to home. It describes how easy something is to use, not that it just got you out of trouble. C is the chunk's other phrase, tame ni naru. That one is for a book, a talk or a show that does you good over time, the way Kenta describes the cookbook in line 3. An umbrella can help you, but it doesn't teach you anything. Lines 3 and 7 already set the two apart: the book is tame ni naru, and the umbrella is yaku ni tatsu.
5. Say it's worth reading with tame ni naru, keeping ni: un, sugoku tame ni naru yo. Before a noun it becomes tame ni naru hon. For example: *un, sugoku tame ni naru yo.* (Yeah, it's really worthwhile.); *un, kekkou tame ni naru hon da yo.* (Yeah, it's a pretty worthwhile book.). Kenta says the same thing about the cookbook in line 3 (kekkou tame ni naru n da yo). Sora turns it round in line 4 (yonde mo tame ni naranai naa) and again in line 16 (boku no tame ni mo naru ne). Tame ni naru says a book, show or talk is good for you, something you come away from knowing more. Yaku ni tatsu is practical help with a task, like the umbrella in line 13. Ni stays even in casual speech. This is not the grammar -tame ni (in order to): here tame means benefit, and the phrase ends in naru.
