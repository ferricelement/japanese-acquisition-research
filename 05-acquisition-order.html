Audio-first Japanese / item-level sequencing

Order by fanout, gate on the ear

Twenty-one stages with measurable entry triggers and exit tests, derived from eleven ordering laws. Grammar is a dependency graph, not a list — sort it by downstream subtree, but never open a node before its induction base exists, and never admit a card before the contrasts that distinguish words are categorical percepts.

Grammar retires — month 9, on a 140-item audio battery 6,000 words — month 12–13

01

The thesis

Order by downstream fanout, not by difficulty and not by category — but gate every rule on an induction base and every card on a validated ear. An SRS review strengthens the phonological trace you held at encoding; it does not repair it. If kite / kiite / kitte collapse into one blurred form, you have not stored a fuzzy word — you have stored the same form three times with contradictory meanings, and every review deepens the collision.

That gives one braid: roughly two weeks of pure discrimination before card one, a vocabulary track capped at 20 new cards/day, a closed-set grammar sprint that opens on an induction base at week 6 and retires on a test at month 9, and an input regime that ignites on a timed comprehension test at ~350 morae/min rather than on a word count.

Where the dependency graph and the interference analysis conflict — potential and transitivity pairs both want the -eru slot, relative clauses and nominalization both want to be first — the tiebreak is always the same: the ear has one cue, so the item whose disambiguator survives in the audio stream goes first, and its homophone waits five weeks.

Unlocking a node with fanout N does not add one construction to your output. It converts N constructions in the incoming stream from noise into structure.
02

Verb morphology is five keys, not fifty patterns

Everything textbooks present as dozens of separate grammar points is five stem operations plus their tails. Sorting by subtree size puts them in an order no textbook uses.

Two consequences fall straight out. The -te chant gets the largest single time budget in the course and lands the week its induction base closes. And naming the i-stem at week 6 — where it is free, because you need it for the -masu shell anyway — converts twenty later "grammar points" into one morphological operation plus twenty tails: -tai, -nagara, -ni iku, -kata, -sugiru, -yasui, -nikui, -hajimeru, -dasu, -owaru, -tsuzukeru, -naosu, -komu, -soo, -nasai.

03

The eleven ordering laws

These generate the sequence. If you disagree with a stage, the productive move is to argue with the law behind it rather than the stage itself.

L1

Perception gates encoding

StatementNo lexical item is admitted until the contrasts that distinguish words are categorical percepts. Vowel length, gemination and moraic N gate absolutely. Pitch accent does not gate — but it is a card-format constraint from card one.

MechanismLength is phonemic and functionally loaded (ojisan/ojiisan, shitte/shite, biru/biiru, kookoo/koko), so collapse destroys lexical identity rather than naturalness. Accent is different in kind: a per-word lexical property, acquired far more cheaply distributed across 6,000 items than concentrated into 60 pairs — but absent at first encoding it costs a second full pass over the lexicon plus a re-recording of the deck.

Consequence10–14 days of discrimination before card one, hard ceiling four weeks, tiered exit: ≥85% on novel talkers releases; 75–85% releases with a continuing 10 min/day track; below 75% at four weeks releases anyway with monthly re-test. Every noun is recorded as X ga from item one, because heiban and odaka are acoustically identical on the bare word. And the TTS pipeline is validated against an accent reference before a single card is generated.

L2

Fanout-weighted topological sort, with a bootstrapping override

StatementAmong nodes whose prerequisites are satisfied, take the one maximising (subtree size × conversational token frequency). Never the easiest, never the one that completes a tidy category. Override: items that let you extract further items from a speaker in real time jump the queue regardless of their own frequency.

MechanismGrammar for a listener is a gating problem, not an accumulation problem. Separately, X tte nani? and nihongo de nan te iimasu ka change the shape of the acquisition curve rather than a point on it: without them vocabulary arrives only from prepared material; with them every conversation becomes an item source.

ConsequenceX tte nani? is item ~30 — roughly 400 items before the quotative -tte it contains. Adjective inflection loses every scheduling contest to the -te subtree, except its -ku/-kunai core, which is pulled forward because six later nodes need it.

L3

The citation form is the form in the signal

StatementWhatever sits on the prompt side gets automatized, and the stream contains the reduced form. So the contraction is the citation form and the long form is the labelled formal expansion.

MechanismA reader can afford the long form because the page always shows it. A listener cannot: training on -te iru inserts a decompression step into every act of listening, and decompression fails first under load. Reductions come in two perceptually distinct kinds — mora-deleting (te iru › teru, te shimau › chau, te oku › toku) and mora-preserving segmental substitution (wakaranai › wakannai, suru no › sun no, samui › samii). Only the first is a shortening; the second keeps the rhythm identical and needs a different drill entirely.

L4

One grammar, two shells, split by channel

StatementTeach the plain grammar once. Comprehend plain from the first verb; produce -masu/-desu for four months. Politeness is a suffix shell, not a second curriculum.

MechanismPoliteness surfaces almost entirely on the final predicate, so clause-internal structure is register-invariant. But the usual claim is overstated and worth stating correctly: plain is obligatory in prenominal, quotative, nominalized and conditional/temporal positions — about 25 constructions — and is fully optional before kara, node, kedo, ga, shi. Ame ga furimasu kara, yamemashoo is grammatical.

ConsequenceCuts roughly 40% off the card count, which is what makes a four-month sprint arithmetically possible.

L5

Contrast to define; separate to encode; frame when both

StatementItems whose meaning is the opposition must be co-taught in one unit. Items sharing a sound shape must be admitted one at a time behind a retrieval-strength gate. When an item is both, no spacing saves you — encode it in a frame whose particle supplies a non-phonological cue.

MechanismA one-member category has no boundary, so the learner fills it from L1: wa taught alone becomes "the subject slot," and because bare wa is almost never ungrammatical in a main clause there is zero negative evidence — every fluent-sounding sentence reinforces the wrong hypothesis for years. The hard case is the -eru family, where potential, transitive, passive and honorific wear one shape. Inflection cannot separate them, because the potential of a godan verb is itself an ichidan verb with an identical -te form. The surviving cue is argument structure: potential takes ga, transitive takes o.

ConsequenceAccent does part of this work for free — kaeru [1] vs kaeru [0], kiru [1] vs kiru [0] are accent minimal pairs, so they train in the phonology block instead of being deferred by months. Only accent-identical collisions get separation.

L6

Rules front-loaded, but never before their induction base

StatementA multiplier enters as soon as ~40 applicable bases exist, presented with 12–15 attested types spanning varied bases before extension is licensed.

MechanismMorphological patterns become productive as a function of type frequency, not token frequency. The corollary bites hardest on the -te chant: the standard induction set (taberu, iku, suru, kuru) contains one ichidan verb, both irregulars, and the single verb that violates the -ku › -ite rule — not one regular godan verb. It cannot possibly teach the five-way alternation.

ConsequenceThe grammar gate is an induction base, not a word count: ≥500 recognized words including ≥40 verbs already stored as (dictionary, -te) audio pairs, ≥4 per godan coda class, with iku deliberately excluded and taught afterward as the sole exception.

L7

Closed sets sprint and retire on a test; open sets change source and never stop

StatementEvery closed set gets a measurable exit condition and a hard calendar ceiling. Every open set switches supply rather than terminating.

MechanismPast roughly 90% comprehension of the core closed set, remaining patterns are rare enough that their expected encounter rate in ordinary input exceeds any plausible drilling efficiency. Syllabi have no terminal point; publishers do, which is why the N4→N3→N2 regress consumes years. The hard ceiling exists because failing the exit test is usually evidence of a speed deficit, and more drilling cannot touch speed.

ConsequenceBut the closed set has to actually be closed. The conventional list omits adjective inflection, the volitional, the request and prohibition paradigm, obligation forms, comparison, question words including nande, and the entire numeral-and-counter system.

L8

Review load is the schedule — and it obeys a different formula than everyone uses

StatementSteady-state reviews/day ≈ 6–7 × new cards/day, not new-per-day × the sum of reciprocal intervals.

MechanismA card occupies interval step Ik for Ik days and generates exactly one review to leave it, so each step contributes N reviews/day once filled. Reviews scale with the count of ladder steps reached, not the sum of their reciprocals. Sanity check: ladder {1 day, then 100 days}, N=1. The reciprocal model predicts ~1 review/day; reality after 1,000 days is ~10.

ConsequenceN caps at 20/day, ramping 10 → 15 → 20. Six thousand items lands month 12–13, not month 9. Roughly a quarter of them arrive free from multipliers rather than from cards, which is what makes the target reachable at all. The taper at month 12 is a cliff, not a glide — short-interval steps carry almost all the cost, and a glide keeps refilling them.

L9

Coverage gates whether input is acquisition; latency gates whether it is comprehension

StatementBelow ~95% lemma coverage, input is decoration. Above it, the binding constraint stops being what you know and becomes how fast you retrieve it. The metric must switch at that point.

MechanismWord counts are the wrong trigger — two learners with identical decks can differ by 40 points on running speech. Japanese conversation runs ~7–8 morae/second, i.e. 420–480 morae/min, not the ~250 that graded material uses, and lexical access must complete in a few hundred milliseconds or the parse falls behind and collapses. A flashcard gives you five seconds and rewards you for using them.

ConsequenceInput ignition fires on a comprehension test at ~350 morae/min, not on a word count. Card latency is measured from clip offset, not onset. Hard rule: if listening comprehension does not improve for six consecutive weeks while vocabulary grows by 1,000+, halve new cards that week.

L10

Head-final parsing is capacity, not knowledge

StatementNoun-modifying clauses, embedded questions and the clause-final predicate complex respond to time-on-task, not explanation. Budget 3–5× what a textbook does, and start exposure well before instruction.

MechanismEnglish is head-initial and parsed incrementally — "the book [that I bought]" lets you attach the head before the modifier arrives. Japanese is head-final: kinoo katta hon forces you to hold an unresolved, complete-sounding predicate in working memory with no relative pronoun, no comma, and in speech no prosodic break. Worse, tense, negation, modality, evidentiality and politeness all stack clause-finally (tabenakatta n ja nai ka to omou). This is the one place audio-first is materially harder than reading-first, because the page supplies word boundaries and the option to re-read.

ConsequenceRelative clauses precede nominalization and quotation — [clause]+no, [clause]+koto, ka doo ka and N to iu N are all the same prenominal parse, so the conventional order puts the dependent before its prerequisite.

L11

The card unit is whatever the ear can identify

StatementMerge orthographic splits that are phonologically and accentually identical; split phonological near-pairs; and for the Sino-Japanese half of the lexicon, the card unit is a collocation, not a word.

MechanismA reader disambiguates kikan (period / institution / engine) by kanji. An ear-only learner has nothing. Kango is 2-morae × 2 with heavy long vowels, geminates and moraic N — low distinctiveness, extreme homophone density, exactly the wrong profile. Conversely, three kanji spellings with one sound and one accent are literally one signal, and separate cards produce identical prompts, which is unlearnable by construction and becomes a permanent leech.

ConsequenceWago cards are bare audio; kango cards carry a 2–4 mora frame from the start. The wago:kango ratio runs ~85:15 through the first 1,000 items and flips to ~40:60 by 6,000.

Two things fall out of writing this down, and neither survives contact with the list.

Break 1

The -nu class has exactly one member, so it cannot be induced at all

The plan requires "≥4 regular verbs per godan coda class" before the -te chant opens. For -nu that requirement is unsatisfiable in principle: shinu is the only -nu verb in the modern language. -bu and -gu are thin but workable at four. So the rule must be restated: induce on the six classes that support induction, and memorize shinu as a second exception alongside iku. Two exceptions, not one.

Break 2

The grammar gate's word count contradicts its own card cap

The gate demands ≥500 recognized words by week 6–7. Under the plan's own ramp — 10/day for weeks 3–4, 15/day thereafter — the maximum reachable by end of week 7 is 14×10 + 21×15 = 455. The most emphasized gate in the plan is unreachable at its stated date by its own arithmetic.

The fix is to notice the word count was never the real constraint. At ~30% verb share, verb #56 arrives around day 17–19 of carding — week 5–6, ahead of the word count. So drop the 500 figure as redundant and state the gate purely as what actually binds: hataraku is carded, four-per-class holds on six coda classes, and iku and shinu are quarantined outside the induction set.

05

The sequence, in six arcs

Twenty-one stages. Every entry is a measurable trigger, not a date; every exit is a test. Dates are consequences, not targets.

I · The ear

DAYS 1–16, CEILING WK 4
S0

Pipeline validation and the notation contract

days 1–2
VocabZero. No cards exist yet.
ExitTTS agrees with an accent reference (NHK / OJAD / UniDic accent field) on ≥95% of a 200-item sample. Without this the entire accent-tagging plan is unfalsifiable.
S1–2

The mora grid, then the three length contrasts; devoicing last

days 1–16
OrderIsochrony on all-CV words first, because length is a ratio and needs a stable denominator. Fricative geminates (issho, zasshi, massugu) before stop geminates — audible friction before a beat of silence. Devoicing last, framed as perception with the beat explicitly preserved.
CorpusAll-voiced only: neko, inu, sakana, tamago, kokoro, otoko, kudamono, mado, yama, umi. Not watashi, tomodachi, murasaki — every one ends in a devoicing environment, which is the phenomenon deferred to day 11.
ExitTiered, 4-week ceiling. ≥85% on 100+ trials, novel talkers, accent-matched minimal triples, single-token identification. 75–85% releases with a continuing 10 min/day track. Below 75% at four weeks releases anyway onto a restricted lexicon avoiding length neighbours.

II · The bootstrapping lexicon

WEEKS 3–8
S3

Predicate core, kosoado, and the extraction toolkit

wks 3–4
VocabResponse tokens first — top 20 of any conversational corpus, outside the top 3000 of any written one: un, uun (the highest-frequency minimal pair in the language — yes vs no by length and contour alone), hai, ee, aa, hee, soo, naruhodo, sokka, honto?, majide, tashika ni.
GrammarCopula paradigm. N no N. Final particles taught prosodically, not semantically. Rising terminal intonation IS the plain-speech question marker; ka is the polite variant. Topic wa deliberately withheld.
ExitExtracts 10 unknown nouns from a scripted interlocutor using only X tte nani? and nihongo de nan te iimasu ka — no English. Item ~30 in the deck, ~400 items before the quotative -tte it contains.
S4

wa and ga as one indivisible contrast

wks 4–6
GrammarOne six-day unit, never wa alone. Diagnostic taught operationally: a question word never takes wa and its answer takes ga. ni-existence against de-action in the same session. The ga-marked-object predicates most curricula scatter: suki, kirai, joozu, heta, hoshii, wakaru, dekiru.
Exit≥18/20 including wh-answers, contrastive wa, double-nominatives, and 5 particle-dropped items.
S5

Numbers, counters, and the transaction script

wks 5–8, parallel
Why hereGated on the mora grid, not on grammar — the counter sound changes are the special-mora contrasts already trained. ippon/sanbon/roppon/happon, ikkai/sangai/jukkai, ippun/sanpun/roppun drill as rhythm in twenty minutes.
ScopeTen counters × cells 1–6 plus the nan- interrogative, not 300 items. The -tsu series in full is the escape hatch that makes the tail safely droppable. Lexicalized exceptions drilled explicitly: yoji not *yonji, kuji not *kyuuji, tsuitachi, futsuka, yokka, hatsuka.

III · The five keys

WEEKS 6–14
S6

Keys 1 and 3 — dictionary form, classes, the i-stem named

wks 6–7
GateVerb #56 carded, four-per-class on six coda classes, iku and shinu quarantined.
MoveEvery verb stored as an (dictionary, -te) audio pair — class never computed. One extra syllable of storage buys the elimination of a rule, a diagnostic, and an exception list. Naming the i-stem here, where it is free, converts twenty later grammar points into one operation plus twenty tails.
CorrectionThe -masu shell is not uniformly accented on ma: nomimásu and nomimáshita, but nomimasén and ikimashóo. Three of the five endings break the rule.
S7

Key 2 — the -nai stem

wk 8
RuleThe w-insertion rule stated correctly: any verb whose dictionary form ends in vowel + u — -au, -iu, -ou, -uu — takes -wanai. kau/kawanai, iu/iwanai, omou/omowanai. The conventional statement omits -iu and therefore mis-generates for one of the highest-frequency verbs in speech.
S8

Key 4 — the -te chant

wks 9–10
RowsOrdered audible-cue-first, by the same logic that put fricative geminates before stops: -mu/-bu/-nu › -nde (nasal voicing, audible) before -u/-tsu/-ru › -tte (gemination, a beat of silence). Then -ku › -ite, -gu › -ide, -su › -shite.
Exit-te and -ta for any of 80 unheard verbs under 300 ms at ≥95%. -ta costs nothing — same slot, final vowel e›a.
S9–11

Aspect, adjective inflection, potential

wks 10–14
S9-teru is the citation form. The two readings co-taught in one session, keyed on aktionsart: durative › progressive (tabeteru); punctual › resultant state (shitteru, motteru, sunderu).
S10Pulled forward from its fanout rank because six later nodes need -ku. ii › yoku/yokatta as full suppletion, drilled separately — yokatta alone is a top-30 spoken utterance.
S11Potential six weeks early, and calibrated: ra-nuki (tabereru, mireru) for 1–2 mora stems where it is at parity; -rareru for 3+ mora stems, where *oshiereru is a form the learner will never hear.

IV · Clause building

WEEKS 14–20
S12–13

-tara alone; the -te auxiliaries and benefactive triangle

wks 14–18
Move-tara taught alone and given ten weeks to become the unmarked default before the other three conditionals arrive. -chau with both senses co-taught — as "regret" alone tabechatta reads apologetic; as "completion" alone wasurechatta loses its affect.
S14

Noun-modifying clauses — the ear-hard node

wks 18–20
BudgetFive days plus a permanent background track. Passive exposure began at S10, six weeks before instruction. A truncation drill runs from week 9: hear a clause with the final predicate complex clipped, predict polarity and tense.
Exit≥8/10 content questions on sentences carrying 3+ mora pre-nominal modifiers at 300 morae/min; iku toki vs itta toki 10/10.

06

Contested calls

Ordering questions where two defensible answers exist. Each gets a committed verdict and a statement of what it costs if wrong.

Does the -te form land at day 22 (the pure dependency-graph answer) or week 9?

Week 9. Later than fanout alone would put it, and still roughly six weeks earlier than any textbook.

The fanout argument is right, but day 22 assumes two false things: there is no validated ear at day 22, and the -te alternations are precisely a gemination-and-voicing chant, so drilling them on uncalibrated categories installs the wrong motor program at volume. Decisively, the conventional induction set cannot teach the rule at all.

If wrongFour extra weeks in which a third of verb tokens stay unparseable — real, and the missed exposure is not recoverable. Mitigated because those verbs are stored as (dictionary, -te) pairs from week 5, so the learner already parses tabete and itta before the rule exists. The chant compresses data they already hold, which is why it takes ten days rather than four weeks.

Is the target 6,000 words by month 9 at 30 new cards/day?

No. N caps at 20/day and 6,000 lands month 12–13. The 30/day figure rests on arithmetic wrong by roughly 3×.

At N=30 under the corrected 6–7N model, reviews alone consume over an hour of a 90-minute budget before any input, output or shadowing. Audio-first makes this worse, not better, because Sino-Japanese homophony forces collocation-length cards for a large share of the lexicon.

If wrongIf the true cost is lower, you have headroom and raise N — cheaply corrected. If the plan had kept 30/day, the failure is the single highest-attrition event in self-directed study: around month 9 the review count arrives at three to four times budget, two days get missed, and a 900-card backlog ends the project.

Which corpus ranks the vocabulary, and does it matter?

CEJC (Corpus of Everyday Japanese Conversation), not the CSJ. This corrects an instrument error that would have inverted the list.

The CSJ is roughly three-quarters academic presentation and simulated public speech — spontaneous in the sense of unscripted, not in the sense of conversational. Lecture monologue is exactly the register dense in shikashi, mata, sarani, tsumari and thin in yo ne, kke, -chau, tteka.

If wrongRanking against a lecture corpus while believing it is conversation is the worse error, because it is invisible: the list looks empirically grounded and is systematically wrong at exactly the top of the distribution, where function words and interactional particles live.

Do relative clauses come before or after nominalization and quotation?

Before. Relative clauses weeks 18–20; nominalization and quotation months 5.5–6.5.

The edge runs one way only — nothing in nominalization is a prerequisite for relative clauses. Every curriculum inverts this because nominalization looks like a small vocabulary item on the page while relative clauses look like syntax. By ear the difficulty ranks the other way.

If wrongn da and to omou arrive later than their frequency deserves. Mitigated directly: n da is admitted at week 3 as a frozen four-cell pattern with a scheduled unpack at S15. Chunk-before-parse applied to a grammar point, at a cost of one card.

How much passive and causative, and when?

Month 8, both narrow. Passive productive only in adversative and possessor frames; causative productive only inside -sasete kudasai / -sasete morau. Causative-passive recognition only.

In conversational audio the passive plausibly loses a frequency contest to -chau, which mainstream curricula never teach at all. The functions English speakers reach for the passive to express are handled in Japanese by benefactives and intransitives, both taught earlier here.

If wrongA learner who ends up in a Japanese workplace finds their passive stiff — recoverable in weeks once the need is real. The alternative spends five weeks of a nine-month sprint on constructions that lose to -chau.

07

Where this contradicts the textbooks

Not stylistic disagreement. Each of these is a place the standard order violates a dependency, and the cost is stated.

Standard

Teaches -masu for 8–14 chapters (Genki reveals plain form at ch. 8, Minna at L20), then presents plain form as "casual speech." Same sequence for comprehension and production.

Here

Split by channel. Plain is the comprehension citation form from the first verb; production runs a -masu shell for four months on social-risk grounds.

Cost of standard: every subordinate clause is relearned, so the plain-form chapter feels like restarting. Learners produce *tabemasu toki, *kaimashita hon for months. It also doubles the card count, which is the overhead that makes a four-month sprint impossible.

Standard

Places -te at Genki ch. 6 / Minna L14 — month 3 at classroom pace — as a five-row table keyed to kana columns, induction set taberu, iku, suru, kuru.

Here

Week 9, ten days, drilled to sub-300 ms on ≥4 regular godan verbs per coda class, with iku and shinu excluded and taught as exceptions.

Cost of standard: eight-plus weeks in which a third of spoken verb tokens are unparseable. And the conventional induction set contains no regular godan verb at all, so it produces a memorized table that fails under time pressure — exactly where it matters.

Standard

Treats contractions as slang or an appendix. Most decks put the long form on the answer side.

Here

The contracted form is the citation form, with the long form taught alongside as the formal expansion. A systematizing pass at month 10 splits mora-deleting from mora-preserving reductions.

Cost of standard: catastrophic for a listener specifically — trained representations never match the signal, so every act of listening carries a decompression tax paid under real-time pressure, and decompression fails first under load.

Standard

Orders vocabulary by written frequency — JLPT levels, newspaper corpora, any kanji-graded core 2k/6k deck — and front-loads Sino-Japanese because the word count climbs fast. Splits homophones by kanji.

Here

Ranked against dialogue corpora, ~85:15 wago:kango through the first 1,000, flipping to ~40:60 by 6,000. Merge orthographic splits that are phonologically and accentually identical; split ones that are not.

Cost of standard: you arrive at 2,000 words knowing shikashi, keizai, de aru and not yappari, nanka, -chau, yo ne, kke — and because the missing items are function words, the gap does not close with more listening, it blocks listening. Kanji-driven sense splits produce cards with identical prompts, unlearnable by construction.

Standard

Never terminates grammar, rolling N5→N4→N3→N2 indefinitely. Drills productive keigo as the beginner capstone.

Here

Terminates on a 140-item audio battery, hard ceiling month 9. Keigo split by channel: the receptive service-encounter script at week 5, a year earlier than standard; the productive o-V-ni naru template never drilled.

What this still gets wrong

An adversarial pass ran against every claim above. These survived as genuine defects — reported rather than quietly patched, because several are load-bearing.

StructuralWithin-stage order is still absent

The deepest gap. Stage 17 contains roughly forty items presented as a set — the whole thesis is that sets are not orders. The stage boundaries are ordered; their contents are not. Same in stages 12, 13, 16, 18.

StructuralThe 35 -te dependents are never enumerated or ranked

The entire -te budget is justified by fanout, and then exactly one child is ranked first before the rest scatter across four stages with no application of the fanout rule. Where does -te mo ii sit relative to -te miru, -te oku, -te kara? Unanswered.

CircularThe fanout metric counts a graph the author drew

"~35 direct dependents" is a count over a curriculum-defined notion of direct dependent. The revision from 45 to 35 is a statement about unitization choices, not about Japanese. Neither number is independently checkable, and the largest scheduling decision rests on their ratio to a runner-up that is never named.

BrokenThe -eru disambiguation fails on its own examples

"Potential takes ga, transitive takes o" works for kaeru. It fails for kireru, yakeru, toreru, wareru, nukeru — every one a potential colliding with an intransitive, where both members take ga. Worse, the plan trains the learner to expect particles to be dropped. The real surviving cue is the presence of an animate experiencer, not the object particle — and the five-week separation is asserted with no mechanism.

Missing~25 named items have no position

The i-stem tails are the headline claim of Law 2 and then never scheduled: -nagara, -kata, -ni iku, -sugiru, -yasui, -nikui, -hajimeru, -owaru, -tsuzukeru appear nowhere in the sequence. Same for the ka/mo/demo indefinite paradigm, purpose clauses (tame ni, yoo ni, yoo ni naru), and mama.

Missingiu is pronounced yuu

言う is yuu, 言った is yutta, って言ってた is tte yutteta. The plan leans on -tte harder than on any other item and never mentions that the citation pronunciation of its source verb is not what the learner will hear. Exactly the class of error the plan exists to prevent.

UnderpoweredHalf the exit tests cannot distinguish pass from fail

≥18/20 and ≥16/20 thresholds on 20-item batteries carry a binomial standard error near 10 points. The original 40-trial phonology gate at 90% fails a true-90% learner about 42% of the time. Gates need 100+ trials, or a sequential stopping rule.

RhetoricalEvery "if wrong" is asymmetric by construction

Mild cost if this plan errs, catastrophe if the alternative had been taken — ten times out of ten. That is a signature, not an analysis. The epistemic standard is also applied unevenly: one source's frequency claim is rejected as unsupported while "-chau beats the passive" is asserted with nothing behind it and then used to cut passive instruction.

The strongest objection, steelmanned. A competent pedagogue would not attack any placement. They would attack the premise that placement is the high-variance term — arguing that instructional sequence is a small-effect variable and input volume is a large-effect variable, and that this plan trades the large for the small. Its active-input allocation accumulates perhaps 100 hours of attended listening in nine months; a cruder plan spending 15 minutes on cards and 75 on graded audio from week 3 would accumulate three to four times that, and would likely have better listening at month 9 despite a worse order.

This lands, partly. The 95%-coverage gate that justifies capping input is imported from reading research, and its transfer to listening is contested — speech carries prosodic and interactional cues that text does not. It does not land on the interference work, which is the best-supported part here, nor on reductions-as-citation-form, which is a claim about what the input contains rather than about acquisition order. Net: the ordering is defensible; "order of acquisition is everything" is not. Honestly, the order buys perhaps twenty percent and volume buys the rest.
