# RE-DERIVED DAILY BRAID UNDER THE VOLUME CONCESSION

**Headline finding before anything else:** the trade was never cards-vs-input. With dwell-graded audio cards at ~6 s/review and ~20 s/new, even a 22/day new rate costs ~22 min/day at peak. The plan's 12/20/32-minute input allocation was not caused by the card budget — it was caused by **the coverage gate**, which told the plan that input below ~95% lemma coverage was worthless and therefore not worth buying minutes for. The gate is the bug. Repeal the gate and roughly 50 free minutes/day appear at every phase without cutting a single card in the coverage-critical zone.

---

## 1. THE ACTUAL EVIDENCE ON COVERAGE THRESHOLDS FOR LISTENING

### 1a. Established (direct empirical work, listening as the criterion task)

| Study | Design | Finding |
|---|---|---|
| **van Zeeland & Schmitt (2013)**, *Applied Linguistics* 34(4):457–479 | Nonword substitution in narrative listening at 90/95/98/100% coverage, n=40 | **90% coverage → mean comprehension 73.5%. 95% → 76%.** Statistically similar. At 100%, 29/40 scored perfect, none below 7/10. They recommend 95% not because 90% fails but because comprehension at 95% is *more consistent across listeners*. |
| **Bonk (2000)** | Listening + coverage measurement across four texts | **No coverage figure guaranteed comprehension.** Some listeners had good comprehension at 80–89%. Below ~80%, good comprehension becomes very unlikely. The required coverage is a **function of the comprehension criterion you demand** — 98% is needed only if you demand ≥60% on a detail-level test. |
| **Adolphs & Schmitt (2003)**, CANCODE | Coverage profile of spoken discourse | 2,000 word families < 95%; **3,000 families ≈ 96%.** The marginal return collapses after 2–3k in speech far faster than in writing. |
| **Nation (2006)** | Wellington spoken corpus | 3,000 families + proper nouns ≈ **95%** of unscripted spoken English; 6,000–7,000 ≈ **98%**. |
| **Stæhr (2009)**, *SSLA* 31(4) | 115 advanced Danish EFL, listening test | Vocabulary breadth predicted **49% of listening variance**. Big jump 3,000 (≈94% coverage) → 5,000 (≈98%). |

### 1b. Assumed, not established — and the critic is right about this

1. **The 98% figure is imported.** Hu & Nation (2000) and Laufer & Ravenhorst-Kalovski (2010) are reading studies with reading criteria. Nation's "6,000–7,000 families for spoken text" is **not an empirical listening-comprehension result** — it is the reading-derived 98% criterion applied to a spoken corpus frequency profile. The plan inherited a reading threshold wearing a listening costume.
2. **There is no breakpoint in either modality.** Schmitt, Jiang & Grabe (2011) found the coverage–comprehension relation is essentially **linear** with no step. "Threshold" is a decision rule about acceptable comprehension, not a property of language.
3. **Stæhr's 98% is the one listening result that supports the high figure — and it is the least transferable.** It used an advanced academic listening *test* with detail questions on unfamiliar topics, single pass, no interaction. That is the maximum-difficulty corner of listening. It says nothing about conversation, where the listener can request repair.
4. **Japanese-specific coverage curves for conversation are not published in any form I could locate.** The CEJC (200 h, ~2.4M words, NINJAL, 2022) exists but I found no per-rank coverage table. The English figures are the only available proxy. My working assumption — **marked as approximate and unverified** — is that Japanese conversational coverage at a given lemma rank runs *higher* than English at low ranks (heavier functional-morpheme token share, extremely formulaic backchanneling) and *lower* at high ranks (kango compounding is productive, so the tail is fatter). Net: "~3,000 lemmas ≈ 95% of conversation" is a reasonable but untested import.

### 1c. The defensible lower threshold

**~90% coverage of the specific text, for adequate gist of familiar-topic conversational or narrative speech.** (van Zeeland & Schmitt: 73.5% mean comprehension.) **~80% is the floor** below which comprehension collapses (Bonk).

And here is the move the original plan missed entirely:

> **Coverage is a property of a TEXT, not of a learner.** 90% per-text coverage is reachable at N=800 by narrowing the topic and repeating the text. It does not require waiting for N=3,000.

Chang & Read (2006) is the direct empirical support: of four pre-listening supports, **topic preparation was the most effective and vocabulary pre-teaching was the least effective**, with repeated input close behind topic preparation. The plan's core mechanism for making input comprehensible (pre-teach the words via cards) is the one that tested worst. Narrowing and repeating tested best.

Concretely, this is why 90% per-text coverage is cheap in Japanese. The following ~100 items plausibly carry **50–60% of conversational tokens** under short-unit tokenization (*approximate — extrapolated from BCCWJ short-unit distributions, not measured on CEJC*):

### 1d. What comprehensible-input theory says about input BELOW the threshold

Krashen's model has no coverage threshold. His construct is comprehensibility achieved *by any means* — context, gesture, pictures, prior knowledge, redundancy, repetition. The coverage literature measures only the **lexical** route to comprehensibility and is silent on the rest. That is a real gap in the plan's reasoning, but it is not a licence for unlimited optimism.

The honest position: **below-threshold input does not do comprehension work, but it does other work that this learner desperately needs, and that no card can do.**

- **Segmentation via statistical learning.** Saffran, Aslin & Newport (1996): 8-month-olds extracted word boundaries from a synthesized syllable stream in two minutes, using transitional probabilities alone, with zero semantics. Adults do this too. Segmenting the Japanese speech stream is a distributional achievement, not a lexical one.
- **Phonetic category formation.** High-variability phonetic training (Logan, Lively & Pisoni, 1991) improves category boundaries using **non-comprehended** stimuli — and requires *many talkers*, not many hours with one.
- **Rhythmic segmentation routine.** Otake, Hatano, Cutler & Mehler (1993): Japanese listeners segment by mora. Installing a moraic segmentation routine in an English-L1 adult is exactly this plan's foundational bet, and it is a perceptual achievement acquired from input, not from cards.
- **Prosodic and phrasal chunking**, including where turn boundaries fall — inaudible in any card.

**The hard limit:** none of that happens ambiently. The decades-of-TV counterexample is real, and Schmidt's noticing hypothesis is the reason. Statistical-learning studies use *attended, structured* input. So: "input below threshold is valuable" is true **only** with the qualifier "for perception, when attended, under a task." That qualifier is the entire content of §4.

**One finding that cuts the other way and partially defends the original plan:** Vidal (2011) found **reading produces greater incidental vocabulary gains than listening at every proficiency level, with the gap largest at low proficiency**. This learner has no reading channel at all. So the input's *vocabulary* yield will sit at the low end of what the English literature predicts. The volume argument survives — but its lexical component is weaker for an audio-only learner than the critic's framing implies. This is why the revision below keeps a **22/day peak** in the coverage-critical zone rather than collapsing to the critic's flat 15 minutes of cards.

## 2. RE-ALLOCATED DAILY MINUTES

**Planning baseline: 90–100 min/day.** Bad-day fallback 60 min (cut in the order given below). Good-day 120 min (**every** surplus minute goes to Track C).

**Three named input tracks, with different jobs and different gates:**

- **Track C — Comprehension input.** Per-text coverage ≥90%, achieved by narrowing and repeating. Grows comprehension. Mandatory output artifact.
- **Track P — Perceptual input.** Coverage irrelevant. Native speed, multi-talker, multi-party. Grows segmentation, category boundaries, prosody. Mandatory countable tally.
- **Track Z — Ambient.** Budgeted at **zero**. Allowed, never counted, never substituted.

### What gets cut, in bad-day order

1. **The up-front perception block's length** — from a long dedicated stretch to 2 weeks × 30 min, then a decaying maintenance thread (12 → 8 → 4 → 0 min). Justification: high-variability phonetic training plateaus; what generalizes is *talker variety*, not hours. Six voices per contrast beats sixty repetitions of one.
2. **Explicit grammar study** — deleted as a standing budget line. It becomes ~20 min, once, at each of five readiness triggers (§5, L9). Total lifetime cost ≈ 100 minutes.
3. **Isolated production drilling** — shadowing survives (it is input *and* output and it is a comprehension test); decontextualized pattern practice does not.
4. **Card review of the tail** — new-card rate tapers to 8/day from month 13 (§3).
5. **On a 60-min day:** cut Track P first, then shadowing, then cards to reviews-only. **Never cut Track C.** Track C is the one line that is doing the work the concession says is 80% of the outcome.

## 3. WHERE THE 6,000 DATE MOVES

**Do not cut cards flat.** Cut them **by the shape of the coverage curve.** Item #400 buys enormous coverage; item #5,400 buys perhaps 0.02%. So: front-load into the coverage-critical zone, taper hard in the tail where input does the work anyway.

Revised ramp: **12 → 18 → 22 → 18 → 12 → 8** per day.

Cumulative, at 90% adherence:

| Point | Cumulative N | Revised date | Original date |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1,000 items | 1,000 | **week 11** | week 12.5 |
| 2,500 items | 2,500 | **month 5.2** | month 5.6 |
| End wk 20 | 2,218 | — | 1,940 |
| Month 9 | 4,372 | — | ~4,600 |
| **6,000 items** | 6,000 | **month 14–15** | month 12–13 |
| Month 24 | ~8,000 | — | ~9,000–12,500 |

**The 6,000 date slips ~2 months. The 1,000 and 2,500 marks arrive EARLIER.** That is the whole point of tapering rather than flattening. For comparison, the critic's cruder version (flat 10/day after week 10) pushes 6,000 to **month 22** — that is the floor, and it is worse than necessary.

### Is the slip acceptable? Yes, for three reasons, and one caveat.

1. **The coverage math says the tail barely matters for conversation.** Items 3,000→6,000 move spoken coverage from roughly 95% to roughly 98% (Nation 2006, Adolphs & Schmitt 2003). Those 3,000 items buy ~3 percentage points. Items 1–3,000 buy ~95. Arriving at 3,000 two weeks early and at 6,000 two months late is a straightforwardly good trade.
2. **The binding constraint at 6,000 is not pile size, it is retrieval latency under time pressure** — and that is trained by input and conversation, not by adding items. A learner with 6,000 recognizable-in-isolation items and 500 hours of listening is worse in conversation than a learner with 4,500 items and 900 hours.
3. **In the tail, the card stops being the teacher and becomes the guarantee.** Peters & Webb and the audiovisual incidental-uptake literature show input teaches mid-frequency vocabulary directly, just inefficiently. Once N ≈ 3,000 and input is heavy, items 3,000–6,000 arrive from input; the card's job narrows to *guaranteeing* the ones input hasn't delivered yet.

**Caveat, stated honestly:** Vidal (2011) says the incidental channel is weaker for listening than reading, and this learner has *only* listening. So argument (3) is softer here than in an English-reading context. That is exactly why the taper bottoms at 8/day rather than at zero, and why the peak is 22 rather than 20.

**Change the goal metric.** "6,000 words by month X" was never the objective; it is a proxy that has now been shown to trade against the objective. Replace it with:

> **Month 24 target: sustain a 30-minute unprepared conversation with a non-accommodating native speaker on everyday topics, and follow a 3-person unscripted conversation at ≥60% gist.**

N becomes an input to that, not a target in itself.

## 4. WHAT KIND OF INPUT, IN ORDER OF WHEN IT BECOMES USEFUL

Constraint that eliminates most "graded" material: **no reading.** Anything whose comprehensibility depends on subtitles, transcripts, or furigana is unusable. What survives is material where meaning is carried by **voice alone, drawings, gesture, or already-known plot.**

### Tier 1 — usable from day 1 (weeks 1–4)
1. **Comprehensible Japanese** (YouTube, Yuki) — Complete Beginner playlist. Whiteboard drawings carry the meaning; zero English; deliberately slow. The best day-1 resource that exists for a no-reading learner.
2. **Nihongo con Teppei for Beginners** — 3–5 min episodes, 600+ of them, one narrow topic each, all-Japanese, heavy self-repetition, no visuals required. This is the narrow-listening workhorse and it is what Chang & Read's "topic preparation" finding predicts should work best.
3. **Your own TTS card audio, replayed as continuous streams** — 10–15 items looped in their "X ga" frames. Free, given the pipeline. Counts as Track C.
4. **Children's songs / dooyoo** — for mora timing only. **Warning: singing distorts both pitch accent and mora length. Never use as an accent model.**

### Tier 2 — from ~week 6–8 (N ≈ 600–900)
5. Comprehensible Japanese, Beginner tier
6. **Sakura Tips** — short, slow monologue
7. **Bitesize Japanese Podcast** — very short, scripted, narrow
8. **Japanese with Shun** — daily-life monologue, moderate speed
9. **Nihongo Switch** — two hosts, semi-scripted

### Tier 3 — from ~week 14–20 (N ≈ 1,500–2,500)
10. **Yuyu no Nihongo** — natural but deliberate; the key bridge resource
11. **Learn Japanese with Noriko** — clear, topical, long-form
12. **Nihongo con Teppei** (main series) — native-speed rambling monologue
13. **Onomappu** — native speed, strong visual and gestural support
14. **Miku Real Japanese** — natural speed, clear articulation

### Tier 4 — from ~month 5–7 (N ≈ 3,000+)
15. **Slice-of-life anime with mundane settings and adult register**: *Shirokuma Cafe*, *Aggretsuko*, *Hanasaku Iroha*, *Sakura-sou*. **The anime trap is specific and it is not about difficulty — it is about register.** Shonen and fantasy teach *yakuwarigo* (role language): ore-sama, -de gozaru, archaic copulas, -zo/-ze, shouting, invented vocabulary. Reproducing it marks you as strange in a way you cannot hear. Filter by *setting*, not by *level*.
16. **Japanese dubs of shows whose plot you already know.** This is topic preparation — the highest-value listening support Chang & Read tested — available for free.
17. **J-drama with mundane settings**: *Shinya Shokudoo*, *Kodoku no Gurume*. Slow, repetitive frames, adult register.

### Tier 5 — Track P from month 3, Track C from month 7–9. This is the actual target.
18. **Terrace House** — genuinely unscripted, mundane, multi-party, overlapping. The closest publicly available approximation to CEJC-style input.
19. **Native-for-native podcasts**: *Yuru Gengogaku Radio*, *COTEN RADIO*, *Rebuild.fm*. Two-person unscripted, with backchannels, interruptions, false starts, and self-repair — none of which appear in graded material.
20. **Variety / talk shows and YouTube zatsudan streams.**
21. **Live conversation with a human tutor** (iTalki, Cafetalk, HelloTalk), from month 5. The **only** input that adapts to you in real time and the only one that produces negotiation of meaning.

### What makes input "attended" rather than ambient

**Track C — comprehension input. All four required:**
- **Per-text coverage ≥ ~90%.** Operational test: after one pass, you can retell the gist in two English sentences. If you can't, it is not Track C — either narrow further, repeat it, or reclassify it as Track P.
- **A mandatory output artifact**: the 2-sentence English retell, plus 3 Japanese chunks you caught verbatim. Logged.
- **Blocks of 10–20 minutes**, then switch. Attention decays and 40 unbroken minutes is 15 minutes of listening and 25 of drifting.
- **Narrow, then repeat**: stay on one topic or speaker for 3–6 sessions; re-listen to the same episode 2–3× across 3 days. Repetition converts partial parses into full ones (Chang & Read).

**Track P — perceptual input. Coverage is irrelevant, but a countable target is mandatory.** Pick one per session:
1. Count every geminate in 60 seconds (kitte, sakka, itchi, matta, otto)
2. Mark every long vowel (obaasan, ojiisan, biiru, tooru, kookoosee)
3. Tally every **-teru**
4. Tally every **-tte**
5. Tally every **n da / n desu**
6. Tally every backchannel (un, hai, ee, aa, naruhodo, soo desu ne) and note who takes the turn immediately after
7. Shadow 5-second chunks **simultaneously**, not after
8. Count speakers and mark every overlap and interruption

**If you cannot state the tally at the end, it was ambient, not attended. Do not log it.**

**Track Z — ambient.** Budgeted at zero. The honest statement: background Japanese has a small nonzero effect on phonotactic habituation and a large, reliable effect on making you feel like you're studying. Only the second one is dependable.

**The trap, stated precisely:** *Input far above level is decoration when it is asked to do comprehension work, and productive when it is asked to do perception work.* The same Terrace House episode is decoration at month 3 if you watch it "to understand," and genuinely productive at month 3 if you listen to count turn-transitions and catch every -tte. **The variable is the task, not the material.** This is what lets the revision buy 3× the volume without buying 3× the wishful thinking.

## 5. WHAT SURVIVES, WHAT DEMOTES — PER LAW

| # | Law | Verdict |
|---|---|---|
| L1 | Perception block before card 1 | **HARD GATE SURVIVES**, reshaped |
| L2 | Accent integer on every card + "X ga" frame | **SURVIVES UNTOUCHED** |
| L3 | Contractions as citation form | **SURVIVES — PROMOTED to load-bearing** |
| L4 | Reduction findings generally | **SURVIVES — PROMOTED** |
| L5 | Interference-based spacing | **SURVIVES AS HARD** |
| L6 | Conversational-corpus ranking (not written, not CSJ) | **SURVIVES — PROMOTED** |
| L7 | wago:kango ratio; kango collocation frames | **Ratio → descriptive. Frames → HARD.** |
| L8 | Grammar gate at verb #56 + ≥4 godan per coda class | **DEMOTED to soft preference** |
| L9 | Five stem keys at fixed weeks | **Order survives. Calendar demoted.** |
| L10 | New-card ramp 10/15/20, 20 hard cap | **RESHAPED to 12/18/22/18/12/8** |
| L11 | "Below 95% coverage, input is decoration" | **REPEALED** |

**L1 — Perception block. Hard gate survives.** This is the one thing volume cannot fix. Japanese-L1 speakers after decades of English immersion still fail /r/–/l/ discrimination: exposure without perceptual attention does not move category boundaries. If mora timing, length, and moraic N are not installed before card 1, every card trains a wrong representation and the extra 200 hours of input are being poured into a broken parser. *Changes:* shorten the up-front block to 2 weeks × 30 min but make it **recurrent** (12 → 8 → 4 min/day maintenance through week 20), require **≥6 talkers per contrast** (single-talker training does not generalize), and **open Track P input on day 1** — the original plan's zero-input opening weeks have no defense, since perceptual input is not coverage-gated. Drill set, real items:

*Vowel length* — obasan/obaasan · ojisan/ojiisan · kado/kaado · biru/biiru · toru/tooru · yuki/yuuki · sato/satoo · ie/iie · koko/kookoo · shujin/shuujin · byooin/biyooin
*Gemination* — kite/kitte · saka/sakka · buka/bukka · ichi/itchi · mata/matta · oto/otto · kako/kakko · shite/shitte · asari/assari · isho/issho
*Moraic N* — mina/minna · kinen/kin'en · tani/tan'i · konyaku/kon'yaku/konnyaku · senen/sen'en
*Accent, in the "X ga" frame (this is why L2 exists)* — hashi ga (chopsticks / bridge / edge) · ame ga (rain / candy) · kaki ga (oyster / persimmon) · ima ga (now / living room)

**L2 — Accent integer + "X ga" frame. Survives untouched.** A schema decision with ~zero minute cost that never gated anything. Keep exactly. It becomes *more* valuable under heavy input, since input is where accent is consolidated and the card is where it is anchored. Demote only *drilling* of accent, never *recording* of it.

**L3 — Contractions as citation form. Survives and is promoted from stylistic choice to load-bearing.** Under 3× input volume this is the law that protects the entire investment: if the card says *-te iru* and the input says *-teru*, the input is unparseable and the extra 200 hours are wasted. The inventory, in romaji:

1. -te iru → **-teru** / -ten · 2. -te iru n desu → **-ten da / -ten no** · 3. -te shimau → **-chau** (past **-chatta**) · 4. -de shimau → **-jau / -jatta** · 5. -te oku → **-toku** · 6. -de oku → **-doku** · 7. -nakereba (naranai) → **-nakya** · 8. -nakute wa → **-nakucha** · 9. -te wa → **-cha** · 10. to iu → **-tte** · 11. to iu ka → **tteka / tsuka** · 12. no da / no desu → **n da / n desu** · 13. -te iku → **-teku** · 14. -ranai → **-nnai** (wakaranai → **wakannai**, tsumaranai → **tsumannai**) · 15. -ru no → **-n no** (suru no → **sun no**) · 16. sore wa → **sorya** · 17. kore wa → **korya** · 18. de wa → **ja** · 19. -te mo → **-tatte** (iku → **ittatte**) · 20. -reba → **-rya** (sureba → **surya**) · 21. mono → **mon** (da mono → **damon**) · 22. ra-nuki: mirareru → **mireru**, taberareru → **tabereru**

**L4 — Reduction findings. Survives, promoted.** Same argument as L3.

**L5 — Interference-based spacing. Survives as hard.** Tinkham (1993, 1997), Waring (1997), Nakata & Suzuki (2019) all converge: semantically or phonologically clustered presentation *slows* acquisition. This is a **cost-free reordering with a real effect**. Nothing in the volume concession touches it. It is the single best-supported piece of the ordering work and it should be the last thing anyone questions.

**L6 — Conversational-corpus ranking. Survives, promoted.** Under heavy input the ranking must match the input distribution or the cards stop pre-teaching what you are about to hear. And it is the only source for items 41–60 above — the backchannel and filler inventory, which is a large token share of real conversation and which written corpora and the CSJ (3/4 lecture monologue) almost entirely omit.

**L7 — wago:kango. Split verdict.** The 85:15 → 40:60 ratio is **descriptive** — it falls out of the frequency ranking. Do not enforce it *against* the ranking; that would be tail wagging dog. The **2–4 mora collocation frame on kango cards survives as hard**, and gets more important under volume: kango homophony (kooen 公園/講演/後援, kikan 期間/機関/器官) is a purely audio-only problem, and the frame is the only disambiguator available to a learner who cannot see the kanji.

**L8 — Grammar gate at verb #56. Demoted to soft preference.** Pienemann cuts both ways. You cannot teach a stage early — but you also cannot *withhold* a stage the input is already delivering, and the learner meets -te forms in Teppei episode 3. Restated: **the gate governs when you run the explicit induction session, never whether input may contain the form, and it must never delay the input schedule.** If the condition arrives late, input proceeds regardless. The -nu fact survives untouched — *shinu* is the sole member of its coda class, so it is memorized alongside *iku*; that is a fact about the language, not a pedagogical choice.

**L9 — Five stem keys. Order survives, calendar demoted.** The dependency order (dictionary → i-stem → -nai stem → -te → e-stem) is defensible on frequency and morphological grounds. The **week numbers are precisely what the teachability hypothesis says you cannot schedule.** Replace each with an input-derived readiness trigger:

| Stem key | Old gate | New trigger |
|---|---|---|
| Dictionary / classes | week 6 | 300 items carded, ≥25 verbs among them |
| i-stem | weeks 6–7 | can catch **-masu** in a 60-s graded clip, ≥80% |
| -nai stem | week 8 | can catch **-nai / -nakya** in a 60-s graded clip, ≥70% |
| -te form | weeks 9–10 | **can tally -te chunks in a 60-s native clip at ≥70%** |
| e-stem / potential | week 13 | can catch **-eru** potentials in a 60-s native clip, ≥60% |

This makes the input do the scheduling, which is the only scheduler the teachability literature endorses.

**L10 — Card ramp. Reshaped to 12 / 18 / 22 / 18 / 12 / 8**, derived from marginal coverage per item rather than from a flat cap. The peak deliberately exceeds the old 20/day ceiling.

**L11 — "Below 95% coverage, input is decoration." Repealed, not demoted.** Replaced by: (a) Track C requires ≥90% coverage **of the specific text**, achieved by narrowing and repeating, not by waiting for N; (b) coverage is irrelevant for Track P; (c) the only genuine decoration is **below ~80% per-text while attempting a comprehension task** — the Bonk floor.

## 6. HONEST EXPECTED OUTCOME

**Calibration note.** FSI rates Japanese Category IV: ~2,200 classroom hours plus roughly equal self-study, ~4,400 h to ILR 3. But that figure includes kanji and full literacy, plausibly 30–45% of Japanese-specific effort. **This learner is not doing "FSI minus reading time" — they are doing FSI with the single most expensive subsystem deleted.** A spoken-only trajectory should be materially faster in wall-clock hours than the FSI number implies. The original plan underexploited this. All percentages below are **calibrated estimates, not predictions with error bars** — treat them as falsifiable checkpoints, not forecasts.

### Month 9

| | **Revised** (~290 h audio, ~85 h cards/perception, N≈4,400) | **Original** (~100 h audio, N≈4,600) |
|---|---|---|
| Graded/scripted monologue, familiar topic | **85–95% gist** | 50–65% |
| Patient native, 1-on-1, daily topics | **70–80%, sustain 15–20 min** | 35–50%, sustain 5 min |
| Native-speed unscripted 2-person podcast | **30–45%** (topic yes, detail no) | 15–25% |
| 3+ person overlapping conversation | 20–30% | 10–15% |
| Speaking | **CEFR A2+/B1−, ILR 1/1+** | A2, slower retrieval |
| Isolated-card accuracy | ~88% | ~93% |

**The original's specific failure mode at month 9 is nameable:** items scoring ~95% in isolation fail at ~40–50% in stream, because they were never encountered under coarticulation, reduction, or native speech rate. That is the classic audio-Anki gap, and 100 hours of listening is not enough to close it. **The critic is right at month 9, and the margin is large.**

The revision's one clear win *outside* comprehension: **mora timing and length contrasts should be near-native-listener-acceptable**, because L1 was kept as a hard gate and drilled with multi-talker variability. Accent will be roughly right on carded nouns, unreliable on verbs and compounds.

### Month 24

| | **Revised** (~870 h audio, ~1,020–1,100 h total, N≈8,000) | **Original** (~500 h audio, N≈9,000–12,500) |
|---|---|---|
| Graded/clear monologue | ~98% — solved | ~90% |
| Native-speed unscripted 1-on-1, familiar topic | **80–90%** | 60–70% |
| Unscripted 2-person podcast, unfamiliar topic | **60–75%** | 45–60% |
| Multi-party overlapping | **50–65%** | 30–45% |
| Speaking | **B1+/B2−, ILR 2** — sustains unplanned conversation with a *non-accommodating* native | B1−, ILR 1+/2− |
| Recognition vocabulary breadth | lower | **higher — the original's one real win** |

**Named holes at month 24 in both versions**, since the conversational-corpus ranking underweights them: keigo and business register; abstract and technical vocabulary; and everything requiring literacy — the deliberate trade.

### The honest costs of the revision

1. **Vocabulary breadth is lower at every single date.** Negligible at month 9 (~4,400 vs ~4,600); real at month 24 (~8,000 vs ~9,000–12,500). If the goal ever reverts to a word count, the revision loses.
2. **The audio-only incidental channel is weak** (Vidal 2011). The critic's "3–4×" volume argument was built on literature where readers exist; this learner's input yields less vocabulary per hour than that literature implies. The direction of the effect is unchanged; the magnitude is smaller.
3. **Cards are self-verifying. Input is not.** This is the largest execution risk and it did not exist in the card-heavy plan. Seventy-five minutes of nominal listening is trivially faked into 20 minutes of listening and 55 of drifting; 22 minutes of cards is not fakeable. **This is the entire reason Track C and Track P both carry a mandatory output artifact.** Without the retell log and the tally log, the revised plan is strictly worse than the original — it will produce fewer cards and no more comprehension.
4. **Outcome variance is wider.** The card plan has a narrow, mediocre distribution. The revised plan has a wider one with a better mean.

### Falsifiable checkpoints — correct the plan against these, not against the estimates above

| When | Test | Failure → action |
|---|---|---|
| Week 8 | Unheard *Teppei for Beginners* episode → correct 2-sentence gist retell | Per-text coverage too low. Narrow harder; repeat more. |
| Week 20 | Tally -teru in a 60-s native clip, ≥70% accuracy | Reduction ledger isn't landing. Move 8 min from Track C to Track P. |
| Month 5 | 10-min tutor conversation on a prepared topic, zero English | Output is under-fed. Move 10 min from Track P to shadowing/production. |
| Month 9 | Unheard *Yuyu no Nihongo* ≥70% gist **AND** unscripted 2-person native podcast ≥30% gist | If the second is <15%, the "input" was ambient. Audit the tally logs. |
| Month 13 | 30-min unprepared tutor conversation, <5 English words | — |
| Month 24 | Unheard Terrace House episode, 3-person conversation, ≥60% gist | — |

### Bottom line

The revised braid wins at month 9 by a large margin and at month 24 by a moderate one, and it wins **on precisely the metric that was declared the goal**. The original wins on item count, which was never the goal. The ordering work is not discarded — L1 through L7 survive intact or strengthened, and L3/L4/L6 become *more* important under volume, because the reduced, conversational forms are what the extra 200 hours actually consist of. What dies is one law: the imported 95% gate. That single repeal buys almost the entire volume increase, at the cost of two months on a date that does not matter.
